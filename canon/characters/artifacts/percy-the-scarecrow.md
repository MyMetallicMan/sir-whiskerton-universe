# Artifact Bio: The Scarecrow in the Pink Skirt

## IDENTIFICATION

**Name:** The Scarecrow in the Pink Skirt (also known as "Percy" — though the name is unofficial, and the Farmer has never confirmed it)

**Type:** Standing Guard / Beloved Oddity / Accident of Weather

**Location:** The far field, at the edge of the pasture where the wind comes in strongest from the west

**Status:** Standing. Watching. Wearing a faded pink skirt.

**First Documented:** After the Great Storm of [year], when the scarecrow's trousers were lost to the wind and Martha dressed him in something else.

---

## PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

This scarecrow began, like all scarecrows, as a bundle of straw, old clothes, and a wooden cross. His shirt is a faded blue work shirt, missing three buttons. His hat is a worn straw affair with a broken brim. His face is a burlap sack with two dark spots for eyes and a single line of stitching for a mouth — tilted, on one end, into what might be a smile.

He was, for years, dressed in a pair of sturdy brown trousers. Practical. Unremarkable. Exactly what a scarecrow should wear.

And then came the storm.

The Great Storm of [year] — the one that took the roof off the tool shed and sent the chickens into the barn for three days — blew the scarecrow's trousers clean off. They were found, three fields over, wrapped around a fence post. They were not recoverable.

The scarecrow stood trouserless for a week. No one quite knew what to do about it. The Farmer was distracted by the roof. The animals found it funny but did not intervene.

And then Martha — who had come over to help with the storm cleanup, who had noticed the trouserless scarecrow on her way in from the road — went home. And came back the next day with an old faded pink skirt.

It was not a *statement*. It was not a *joke*. It was simply what she had. A skirt she no longer wore. A skirt that would do the job.

She dressed the scarecrow herself. She tied the waist with a length of twine. She adjusted the straw. She stepped back to look at him.

And then she said, quietly, to no one in particular: *"There. That'll do."*

The skirt has been on the scarecrow ever since.

It is now very faded — a pink that has softened, over the years, into something closer to cream. The sun has bleached it. The wind has frayed it. It has been mended twice, by Martha, on visits that were not strictly necessary.

It is, by any measure, extremely effective. The motion of the skirt in the wind keeps the crows away far better than the trousers ever did. It is also, by any measure, the most beautiful thing in the far field.

---

## THE HISTORY

The Farmer has never commented on the skirt. Not once. Not to Martha, not to the animals, not to anyone.

He has, on several occasions, been observed standing at the edge of the far field, looking at the scarecrow. His expression is unreadable. He does not say anything. Then he walks away.

The animals have decided not to ask.

Martha has never explained the skirt either. She has simply continued to visit. She has continued to mend it. She has continued to bring the Farmer pies.

The scarecrow stands in the far field. The skirt blows in the wind. Neither of them says anything.

But the field is protected. The crows stay away. And the farm is, in some small way, more *itself* than it was before.

---

## THE FEELING

Visitors to the far field report a strange combination of emotions. The scarecrow is, on one hand, *ridiculous* — a burlap sack in a faded pink skirt, standing in a field like it doesn't know it's absurd. And on the other hand, it is *beautiful* — a small, quiet thing that no one planned and everyone kept.

The wind moves through the skirt constantly. It *breathes*. It is, in its way, alive.

An hour in the far field feels like a moment. A moment feels like an hour. The scarecrow does not shift.

---

## THE MYSTERY

Does the Farmer know who dressed the scarecrow? Does he mind? Does Martha know he knows?

No one asks. No one needs to.

The scarecrow does not answer. He stands. That is what he does.

---

## THE INVITATION

The far field is open to anyone. The scarecrow is open to no one — or rather, he is open to *everyone*, in the way that a standing post is open.

You are welcome to stand beside him. You are welcome to watch the skirt move in the wind. You are welcome to lean against his post and look out over the pasture.

He will not answer. But he will *stand*. He will *hold*. He will *wear the skirt.*

That is enough.

---

## THE ARTIFACT'S PLACE

The Scarecrow in the Pink Skirt is a *quiet love letter*. He is the farm's reminder that some of the most important gestures are the smallest ones — a skirt, tied with twine, on a scarecrow, in a field, on a day no one remembers exactly.

He appears in stories as a still point, a quiet presence, a thing that no one talks about and everyone notices. He is one of several scarecrows on the farm, but he is the only one with a name, and the only one with a skirt, and the only one that the animals have ever caught Martha talking to.

In the Commonplace Books, the scarecrow might appear as:

- A pencil sketch of the scarecrow, the skirt drawn in careful detail, the wind indicated with light strokes
- A pressed wildflower, saved from the far field, with a note: *"He watches."*
- A page of Martha's notes, in her careful hand: *"Mended the skirt again. The wind is hard on it. The Farmer did not mention it. I did not mention it. The field is protected. That is enough."*
- A photograph of the scarecrow at sunset, the skirt glowing faintly pink in the last light

---

## THE FINAL WORD

The scarecrow stands. He wears the skirt. The skirt fades. The skirt blows.

He does not know he is loved. He does not need to. He is *loved anyway*.

Come stand beside him. Watch the skirt move in the wind. Do not ask why it's there. Do not ask who put it there.

Just let it be. Just let the field be.

The skirt is enough. The skirt is always enough.
