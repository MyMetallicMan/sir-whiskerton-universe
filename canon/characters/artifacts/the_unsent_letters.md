# Artifact Bio: The Unsent Letters

## IDENTIFICATION

**Name:** The Unsent Letters (also known as "The Drawer," "The Correspondence," or, in the Farmer's private thoughts, "The Things I Mean To Say")

**Type:** Correspondence / Sacrament / Private Practice

**Location:** The top drawer of the Farmer's battered desk, in the farmhouse — nestled with spare nails and twine

**Status:** Active. Ongoing. Never sent.

**First Documented:** The earliest surviving letter is dated forty summers ago. The Farmer has written more every week since.

---

## PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

The letters are stacked loosely in the top drawer of the Farmer's desk. There is no order to them. No filing system. They are simply *there*, one atop another, accumulating slowly over years.

The paper is ordinary — some of it lined, some of it plain, some of it torn from the margins of old newspapers. The ink is often faded. The handwriting is the Farmer's: loopy, uneven, prone to trailing off at the ends of lines.

The letters are addressed to:

- **Martha.** Many letters. All of them tender. None of them sent.
- **Dad.** The Farmer's father. The letters are short. They often begin with *"Good enough, Dad"* and end without a signature.
- **The Company That Made The Good Shovel.** A letter of appreciation. Never sent. The Farmer considers it the finest thing he has ever written.
- **Li Hua.** His Chinese pen-pal. The letters are the most philosophical of the collection. They are also the most hopeful. *(See [Li Hua](../../humans/li_hua.md) and [The Chinese Student](../../humans/the_chinese_student.md).)*

Other letters, unaddressed or addressed to names no one recognizes, are tucked between them. The drawer is a small, private world.

---

## THE HISTORY

No one knows when the Farmer began writing letters he did not send. The practice seems to have begun in his childhood — one of the earliest letters is addressed to "Dad" and dated forty summers ago.

The letters were never meant to be sent. They were meant to be *written*. The writing is the point. The words are a way of holding a thought, of offering it to someone, of letting it exist.

The Farmer has never spoken of the letters. He does not hide them. He simply *keeps* them.

Martha has seen the drawer, once or twice. She has never opened it. She has never asked.

---

## THE LETTERS

The letters vary in length and tone. Some are short — a single paragraph, a single line. Some are long — pages of meandering thoughts, philosophical musings, and observations about the weather.

All of them are *addressed*. All of them are *written*. None of them are *sent*.

### Letters to Martha

The most numerous. The most tender.

They are not love letters, exactly. They are *life* letters. They describe the farm. They describe the day. They describe the way the light falls in the afternoon, the way the eucalyptus grove has grown, the way the Spring stays cold, the way the chickens have been particularly dramatic lately.

Some of them mention Martha directly. Most of them do not. But all of them are *for* her, in a way that she would recognize if she ever read them.

She has not read them. She has not needed to. She already knows.

### Letters to Dad

The most personal. The shortest.

They are not long, these letters. They are not elaborate. They usually begin with the same phrase — *"Good enough, Dad"* — and they usually end without a signature.

The Farmer's father is dead. The Farmer has not spoken of him often. But the letters continue. The Farmer writes to him weekly. The Farmer does not stop.

The letters are not sad, exactly. They are *conversational*. They are the way the Farmer talks to the people he misses — quietly, without ceremony, without any expectation of a reply.

### The Letter to the Company That Made The Good Shovel

This is the Farmer's finest letter. It is not sentimental. It is not philosophical. It is *practical*. It is a letter of *appreciation* to the company that manufactured a shovel the Farmer has owned for thirty years.

He describes the shovel. He describes the work it has done. He describes the way the handle has worn smooth against his hand, and the way the blade has never once failed him, and the way he intends to keep using it until the day he dies.

He has never sent this letter. He does not need to. He just needed to *write* it.

### Letters to Li Hua

The most philosophical. The most hopeful.

The Farmer has a Chinese pen-pal named Li Hua. He does not know that Li Hua is a fictional character — a name used by generations of Chinese students to practice writing English letters to imaginary foreign friends. He does not need to know.

He writes to Li Hua the way he writes to everyone else — about the weather, the farm, the animals, the moon. He asks questions. He offers observations. He ends his letters with *"I hope this finds you well."*

He has never sent these letters either. But somehow — impossibly — he feels that Li Hua has *received* them.

He has never explained this feeling. He has never needed to.

---

## THE FEELING

The letters have no feeling to an outside observer. They are paper. They are ink. They are words. They are not alive.

But the Farmer's desk, where the letters live, has a *stillness* to it. It is a place where things are held. It is a place where things are not rushed.

If you sit at the desk — as Martha has, once or twice — you can feel it. The letters are not sad. They are not regretful. They are simply *there*. A record of someone who has felt a great deal and chosen to write it down, even if he never shares it.

The desk is a *quiet place*. It is the Farmer's most private room, in a house that has few private rooms.

---

## THE MYSTERY

**Will the letters ever be sent?** No one knows. The Farmer has never said. He may never say.

**Has the Farmer ever sent *any* of them?** No one knows. The letters are never stamped. The letters are never mailed. But the drawer is large, and the Farmer's memory is unreliable, and there is a small chance — a very small chance — that one letter slipped out, once, and was posted, and was never spoken of again.

The Farmer has never commented on this. He has never needed to.

**What would happen if Martha read them?** No one knows. Martha has never read them. Martha has never needed to. She already knows what they say.

**What would happen if Li Hua replied?** No one knows. Li Hua has never replied. Li Hua does not need to. The Farmer already feels that Li Hua has *received* his letters. This is the same, and different, and no one can explain how.

---

## THE INVITATION

The letters are not for visitors. They are for the Farmer. The desk drawer is his. No one opens it. No one asks to.

But if you are in the barn, and the Farmer is nearby, and he is sitting at his desk, and the drawer is closed, and he is writing — *do not interrupt*.

Let him write. Let him finish. Let him close the drawer without sealing the envelope.

The letters are not for you. But they are *because of you*. They are because of everyone the Farmer loves, and everyone he has ever wanted to reach, and everyone he has not yet found the words for.

---

## THE ARTIFACT'S PLACE

The Unsent Letters are a **practice**. They are the farm's reminder that writing is a way of loving, that words matter even when they are not spoken, that some of the most important things are said to no one.

They appear in stories as:

- **A quiet motif** — the drawer is always closed; the practice is never explained
- **A source of tenderness** — when characters learn about the letters, they understand the Farmer a little better
- **A bridge to the distant** — the letters to Li Hua connect the farm to a world beyond it

In the Commonplace Books, the letters might appear as:

- A single letter, reproduced in the Farmer's handwriting, addressed to "Dad" and beginning *"Good enough."*
- A page from Martha's own notebook, in response to a letter she never received: *"I would have written back."*
- A reproduction of the letter to the Company That Made The Good Shovel, with a note: *"I never sent this. It didn't need to be sent. It just needed to be written."*
- A photograph of the desk drawer, closed, taken by someone who did not open it
- **A single page, tucked into the back of the Commonplace Book**, with a note: *"I wrote a letter once. I never sent it. I don't remember what it said. I remember that I meant it."*

---

## THE FINAL WORD

The letters are unsent. They are unwritten back to. They are unread by anyone but the Farmer.

But they are *there*. They are a small, private world, held inside a drawer, waiting for a moment that may never come.

Come sit near the desk. Do not open the drawer. Do not ask about the letters.

Just let them be. Just let the Farmer be.

The writing is the sacrament. The sending is irrelevant.
