# Artifact Bio: The Unsent Letters

## IDENTIFICATION

**Name:** The Unsent Letters

**Type:** Correspondence / Sacrament / Collection

**Location:** The top drawer of the Farmer's battered desk, nestled with spare nails and twine

**Status:** Active. Ongoing. Never sent.

**First Documented:** No one knows when the first letter was written. There are letters to people the Farmer has never met, and letters to people he has loved for decades.

---

## PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

The letters are stacked loosely in the top drawer of the Farmer's desk. There is no order to them. No filing system. They are simply *there*, one atop another, accumulating slowly over years.

The paper is ordinary — some of it lined, some of it plain, some of it torn from the margins of old newspapers. The ink is often faded. The handwriting is the Farmer's: loopy, uneven, prone to trailing off at the ends of lines.

The letters are addressed to:

- **Martha.** Many letters. All of them tender. None of them sent.
- **Dad.** The Farmer's father. The letters are short. They often begin with *"Good enough, Dad"* and end without a signature.
- **The Company That Made The Good Shovel.** A letter of appreciation. Never sent. The Farmer considers it the finest thing he has ever written.
- **Li Hua.** His Chinese pen-pal. The letters are the most philosophical of the collection. They are also the most hopeful.

Other letters, unaddressed or addressed to names no one recognizes, are tucked between them. The drawer is a small, private world.

---

## THE HISTORY

No one knows when the Farmer began writing letters he did not send. The practice seems to have begun in his childhood — one of the earliest letters is addressed to "Dad" and dated forty summers ago.

The letters were never meant to be sent. They were meant to be *written*. The writing is the point. The words are a way of holding a thought, of offering it to someone, of letting it exist.

The Farmer has never spoken of the letters. He does not hide them. He simply keeps them.

---

## THE FEELING

The letters have no feeling to an outside observer. They are paper. They are ink. They are words. They are not alive.

But the Farmer's desk, where the letters live, has a *stillness* to it. It is a place where things are held. It is a place where things are not rushed.

If you sit at the desk — as Martha has, once or twice — you can feel it. The letters are not sad. They are not regretful. They are simply *there*. A record of someone who has felt a great deal and chosen to write it down, even if he never shares it.

The desk is a *quiet place*. It is the Farmer's most private room, in a house that has few private rooms.

---

## THE MYSTERY

Will the letters ever be sent? No one knows. The Farmer has never said. He may never say.

The letters are a *sacrament*. They are a promise. They are a small, private act of faith — the belief that words matter even when no one reads them.

---

## THE INVITATION

The letters are not for visitors. They are for the Farmer. The desk drawer is his. No one opens it. No one asks to.

But if you are in the barn, and the Farmer is nearby, and he is sitting at his desk, and the drawer is closed, and he is writing — *do not interrupt*.

Let him write. Let him finish. Let him close the drawer without sealing the envelope.

The letters are not for you. But they are *because of you*. They are because of everyone the Farmer loves, and everyone he has ever wanted to reach, and everyone he has not yet found the words for.

---

## THE ARTIFACT'S PLACE

The Unsent Letters are a *practice*. They are the farm's reminder that writing is a way of loving, that words matter even when they are not spoken, that some of the most important things are said to no one.

They appear in stories as a quiet motif — a drawer that is always closed, a habit that is never explained, a small act of faith that no one observes.

In the Commonplace Books, the letters might appear as:

- A single letter, reproduced in the Farmer's handwriting, addressed to "Dad" and beginning *"Good enough."*
- A page from Martha's own notebook, in response to a letter she never received: *"I would have written back."*
- A reproduction of the letter to the Company That Made The Good Shovel, with a note: *"I never sent this. It doesn't need to be sent. It just needed to be written."*
- A photograph of the desk drawer, closed, taken by someone who did not open it

---

## THE FINAL WORD

The letters are unsent. They are unwritten back to. They are unread by anyone but the Farmer.

But they are *there*. They are a small, private world, held inside a drawer, waiting for a moment that may never come.

Come sit near the desk. Do not open the drawer. Do not ask about the letters.

Just let them be. Just let the Farmer be.

The writing is the sacrament. The sending is irrelevant.
