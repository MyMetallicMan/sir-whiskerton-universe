# My Writing Philosophy

I love writing.

I love literature. I never studied it formally in a university. Instead, I adopted a love for it through living life. One story. One poem. One day at a time.

I grew up reading books. From the reprints of the Doc Savage novels to the space soap operas of Commander Grimes. From the anthologies of science fiction short stories, to the large involved novels of Robert Heinlein, and the prose of Ray Bradbury. From the beauty of poetry of Robert Frost, to the brilliance of Samuel Taylor Coleridge. I absorbed it all.

I never, ever thought about becoming a writer.

It just happened.

---

## Where I Come From

To understand my writing philosophy, one must see the lens of reality that I experienced.

I am an American, and I grew up in the 1960s. It was a landscape of change, and uniqueness that no longer exists. It's gone, but lives on in my own memories.

I attended high school in the 1970s. With my father working in a steel mill. My mother was a housewife. Well, at least until the "Woman's movement" hit town. At that time, we lived in the Pittsburgh area — Western Pennsylvania. I was an "Iron Steel Baby." It was a small town; East Brady. A mining and farming town on the banks of the Allegheny river.

When I was little, I played cops and robbers. I pretended that the sidewalks were rivers of quicksand, and trees were rocket ships. I played cowboys and Indians. I climbed cliffs and splashed in forest streams with lichen growing between the cool gray boulders. During the long hot summers I would ride my banana-seat Schwinn bicycle along old disused rail tracks that wound in and through hilly wood-covered hills, cool rural brooks and refreshed my self by drinking from the springs that seemed to be everywhere. I had a tree house. It was in a large maple tree. I had a cat named Sedgwick. He was a good cat. He lived to a ripe old age. He deserves to be remembered. I also had a husky. Belle. She also was good. I would take her for a walk, though it looked like she was the one walking me.

I had chores from the time I was five years old.

I started working at fourteen. I stocked shelves and bagged groceries. I wore a white short-sleeved shirt and a red bow tie. I worked in coalmines. I worked in steel mills. I fought forest fires.

I was typical for that place, at that time.

I went to college. I had great dreams. I had great plans and a bright future. And I landed a "good job" with a promising company… But, as we know, the industrial landscape started to change drastically in the late 1970's into the 1980's.

Instead of a life that mirrored my father's, I ended up in a blue and beige cubicle, facing a glowing computer monitor, toiling under fluorescent lights. I wore a corporate uniform. You know it, don't you? White short sleeve shirt. Red tie. Beige trousers. Identification badge clipped on a lanyard around my neck.

Computer monitor. Cup of coffee. Papers, and a pen. Spreadsheet.

That is where Sir Whiskerton was born.

## What I Learned About Writing

I learned poetry by force.

The nuns made me memorize it. I cried when I tried to memorize the lines. I protested when my father tried to help me. Oh, it was a brutal event. Every night. Memorization. I hated it then. I despised it then.

I love it now.

Robert Frost. "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood."

I did not understand it as a child. It was just a task, and uncomfortable task. One of strife, and great discomfort. I just couldn't understand why I was learning it. I did not understand the poem at all.

I understand it now.

We choose paths. The paths change us. And we look back and wonder.

…

Samuel Taylor Coleridge. "In Xanadu did Kubla Khan / A stately pleasure-dome decree." I did not understand it as a child.

Oh, for certain, I understand it now.

The vision. The interruption.

And, the fragments that remain.

…

Poetry taught me rhythm. Poetry taught me weight. Poetry taught me that a single word can stand alone and mean everything.

Everything.

I also learned from living. I learned from working.

But my biggest lessons came from how I was treated. I learned from losing jobs on Christmas Eve. And, I learned from building a world that no one; absolutely no one, could take away from me.

---

## Why I Write

I write because I need to.

I write because the world is loud and I need a place that is quiet.

I write because I have seen things that cannot be unseen, and I need to put them somewhere.

I write because I built a farm — a fictional farm, that is a real farm in my heart; in my soul and in my very being. It is a farm of the heart — and I need to tend it.

I write because the lost belong somewhere, and I need to build that place, somewhere.

I write because I am not a writer.

I am a survivor.

---

## How I Write

It's not too complicated.

I sit down. I find a subject, and I start writing.

It does not matter where it goes. It does not matter how it flows. It does not matter whether I can make money from it.

The writing is what matters.

It's an action. Not a result.

Some days the words come easily. Some days they do not. Some days I write a single sentence and stare at it for an hour. Some days I write a thousand words and delete them all.

But I sit down. I find a subject. I start writing.

That is the philosophy.

That is the whole of it.

---

## What I Believe About Writing

I believe that writing is not about rules. It is about rhythm.

I believe that short sentences have weight.

Each. Word. Matters.

…

I believe that periods are stronger than commas.

The pause. The breath. The reader stops.

…

I believe that repetition is not laziness.

It is emphasis. It is a mantra. It is a way of speaking truth into being.

…

I believe that three-beat phrases land like hammers.

Power. Passion. Purpose.

…

I believe that writing is not only communication.

It is performance, and the performance is often the only truth that matters.

…

I believe that you do not need to be near to be near. You do not need to be the same to belong. You do not need to speak loudly to be heard.

You just need to be you.

…

I believe that the most powerful force is not a loud demand but a patient, undeniable presence.

I believe that belonging is not something you earn. It is something you accept.

I believe that homes are not sold. They are kept.

---

## What I Reject

I reject the idea that writing must follow a formula.

I reject the idea that writing must be profitable to be worthwhile.

I reject the idea that AI can replace the human voice. I have seen what happens when AI touches my stories. It turns up the volume. It adds conflict. It modernizes. It makes my quiet world loud. It turns a flower swaying in the breeze into the cracked cement of a dirty industrial hub.

I reject the Optimizer-Defiler — the MBA, the developer, the streaming executive — who will one day discover my world and try to "improve it", and turn it into something it is not, and what it was never intended to be.

I reject the idea that I need anyone's permission to write.

---

## What I Embrace

I embrace the quiet.

I embrace the pause.

I embrace the space between words.

I embrace the reader who has not yet arrived. The one who — right now, somewhere, in an office, on a bad day, in a life that has been laid off one too many times — needs a door that is open. A place where the lost belong. A farm where the tea is warm and the light is on.

I embrace the act of sitting down. Of finding a subject. Of starting to write.

---

## The Philosophy

I am not a writer in a professional sense. I am not following rules. I am following my love. I am following my ear. And I am doing so on my terms. In my way.

That that, my friend…

That is the mark of a writer.

…

So this is my writing philosophy:

Sit down. Find a subject. Start writing. It does not matter where it goes. It does not matter how it flows. It does not matter whether you can make money from it.

The writing is what matters.

…

Write like a survivor. Write like a navigator. Write like a man who has learned that words are not just words — they are frequency. They are intention. They are the tools of reality navigation.

Your style is not a style. It is a signature. It is the frequency of your soul, encoded in language.

That is why it cannot be replicated.

That is why it is yours.

…

So sit down.

Find a subject.

Start writing.

— Robert
