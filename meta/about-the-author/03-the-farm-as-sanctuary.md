# The Farm as Sanctuary

I remember the day well.

I was at work. It was in a brick building attached to a sprawling pole-building that housed the manufacturing side of my employer's operation. I didn't have a window. That was reserved for "management." I had a cubicle.

It was my domicile.

And that day was memorable.

I had just left a meeting with my supervisor. He wore a yellow tie, on his light blue long-sleeve shirt. And his desk faced the door, and he faced me.

To him, I was simply another corporate worker. White short-sleeved shirt. Red tie. Identification badge on a lanyard around my neck. Beige trousers.

And that day… THAT day… well, it was a day that I will never forget.

For on it, I had just endured a long, detailed talk with my supervisor. And, you know, it did not go well.

You see, I had done something very bad.

Terrible. Actually.

By the way it was presented to me, it was almost like I had single-handedly destroyed the company, and the entire world was up in arms against me.

…

It was bad. Really bad.

My signature wasn't acceptable to management. I had to change it.

It was too messy. It was illegible. It didn't fit their expectations.

It needed to change. And, you know, that wasn't an option. It must change. Must. There wasn't anything else that needed to be said. Change my signature now, or start packing up my stuff.

He had me sign a document where I agreed to change my signature, and my new signature would be reviewed for acceptability before it would be accepted.

And that, was that.

…

Oh, but that wasn't all.

My picture on my desk wasn't acceptable either. It was a picture of my cat, Texie. He was lounging, like all good cats do, under a large bush. It was a nice picture. Black and white tuxedo cat with a lush green background behind.

I was told to remove it.

The only permitted pictures were of my wife or girlfriend. The acceptable sizes were specified in the employee handbook.

He also pointed out that the replacement picture had to be on the left side of the desk so that it was not obvious to those walking past my cubicle. He went in great detail on the reasons behind this, and honestly they seemed to make sense. You know, in a perverted megalomaniac way.

…

Oh, but he was just getting started.

Those weren't the only things wrong with me.

I also had a terrible habit. When I sat at my desk inside my cubicle, I would kick my shoes off and sit there wearing only my socks.

I was told that was quite unprofessional.

I was told that if I needed to do so, I should go to a doctor and be diagnosed. But on my own dime. The company would not foot the bill.

That I should always dress for success.

Nothing short of that mattered.

…

Then, after that preamble, my supervisor launched into a step-by-step breakdown of my project, and why everything wrong with it was my fault.

In hindsight, that was pretty ballsy. Seeing that I was part of a four man team on that project and I was being treated as the one and only "weak link" in what was admittedly a very complex process. He went into great detail why I failed him.

Step by step.

Blow by blow.

He took his time. Spitting out the words like they were fiery drops from a molten volcano. He paused for emphasis each and every time and glared at me.

And then I was berated because I didn't tell him about the problems earlier. Of course it didn't matter that the supervisor was out for the entirety of the last month. As he told me, "Stop trying to place blame elsewhere." It was as if that detail was not a contributor.

As he stated; he had generously given me that project and now he greatly regretted it.

…

Finally, in closing, he told me that my 360 review would be on Friday and that I wasn't looking good. That I needed to "up my game" and to be "more of a team player." For me to stop playing the blame game and accept that I was substandard and less than what he expected.

And at that, he dismissed me.

…

As I walked back from that fiasco, I looked back through the window of his office. I watched him laugh joyously with two others from the company. It was as if my personal, career, and intellectual tear-down was meaningless. Ah. It was just another day at the office.

It was Tuesday.

So I went to my cubicle.

Faced my computer and just sat there.

…

I couldn't focus. I wanted to crawl under a rock and hide. I wanted to go to a bar and get shit-faced drunk. I wanted to cry. I wanted to rage, and I wanted to be left alone.

I pulled up a blank Microsoft Word template and started writing.

And the Sir Whiskerton universe was born.

And you know what?

The more I wrote, the calmer I became.

It was my therapy. And I poured my very heart and soul into it.

With each, and every word, the universe took shape. And I was able to escape into it.

I escaped.

Into.

The world I created.

And that is the truth and the reality behind the Sir Whiskerton universe.

…

And you, the reader who has found this, I write to you; to welcome you.

You are welcome here.

Just the way you are.

Jingle on.

— Robert
