# Why I Built This World

*An author's note. The why behind the farm.*

---

## It Began With a Bad Day

It began with a frustration.

I was at my corporate job. It consisted of a tan and blue cloth covered cubicle with a computer, some binders, and a sterile desk policy. I had a few memos pinned to the walls, and a plastic paperweight with the company logo emblazoned upon it. And that day, I well remember. It was, for truth, a bad day. A really bad day. It was the kind of day where you cannot focus on the things you are supposed to focus on because all the things you are supposed to focus on are killing you slowly.

So I opened a Word document.

On my office computer. After all, inside the bare and desolate wasteland of an "office", there wasn't much else to do.

The computer, you know, was the one thing everyone else could see.

I started writing.

To everyone else, I was working. A very important project. They walked past my desk and saw a man typing with intensity. Of course, they assumed I was deep in something serious. They left me alone.

In truth, and in reality, I was writing my frustrations out.

I imagined myself as a cat. My cat. My black-and-white tuxedo cat named Texie. Though, somehow, by the end of the first page, he had acquired a top hat. A monocle. And, you know, an air of superiority.

Oh, what a cat he was. He watched the crazy world of the farm around him — the hens, the geese, the pig, the dog — the way I watched people scurrying around the office. Each in their own small orbit. Each certain their orbit was the important one.

I wrote for hours.

I wrote for days.

And all that time, the office around me left me alone.

I was free.

---

## It Grew

My little world grew.

It started with a single cat on a single bad day. It grew — over months, over years — into a farm.

Oh, and what a farm it was. It had everything. It had a pond. In the pond were frogs. Eventually, the pond had fish… yodeling fish. The farm had a barn. And in that barn was a hayloft. And hidden in that hayloft was a cat. A cat who kept a journal.

There were hens who gossiped. A rooster who crowed with more pride than sense. A pig with a philosophical streak. A dog who glowed in the dark and did not care why.

My world got larger. And larger.

It stopped being about my bad day. It became something else. Something I did not name for a long time.

It became a place I could return to. A place that was not THE OFFICE. Not the commute. Not the performance review. Not the meeting that should have been an email.

A place where things were quiet. Where the conflicts were small. Where no one was fired. Where no one was laid off.

Where everyone belonged. Where everyone was accepted. Where everyone was protected.

---

## It Continued Through Layoffs

But that was not my real life.

I was an American. And during the 1990's it was the age of Corporate mergers, hostile takeovers, and Wallstreet driven decisions.

I was laid off.

On Christmas Eve.

Not the first time. It happened seven times before.

Seven.

I'm calling them out for posterity. Edgewater Steel. Delco Electronics. Magnavox. Poulan Weedeater. Grote. Pollox. And Guardian Glass.

And now yet again. This one; where I was writing my stories, was once again on Christmas Eve.

If you are not American, you may not know this. In America, layoffs happen on Christmas Eve. It is a thing. It is a way of saying: "We are letting you go, and we are doing it on the day you were going to see your family. It is our way of saying: We do not see you employee droid #4528. We do not care about you. But, you can rest assured knowing that the shareholders of the company made a nice profit eating you up and spitting you out."

And as usual, instead of joining the rest of the group at a luncheon, or the pot-luck in the meeting room, I was escorted out by the security guard, carrying my cardboard box full of the few things that mattered to me.

I continued writing when I was unemployed.

I continued writing when I was employed. I continued writing through the interviews. The rejections. The we regret to inform you. The we have decided to move forward with other candidates.

I continued writing through the good jobs and the bad jobs and the jobs that ended without warning.

But, the farm did not lay me off. It never laid me off.

The farm did not have a Christmas Eve.

The farm kept the door open.

The world grew through me. I was not sure, always, what it was for.

I only knew it was mine.

---

## It Met AI (and Was Nearly Ruined)

At some point, I started to play with AI. I asked it to help me with my stories.

The first trials were poor. Very poor, to tell you the truth. The AI did not understand what I was doing. I had backups, thank goodness, and I discarded those early mistakes. But it was a lesson.

AI is great for average.

AI is great for run-of-the-mill.

That's what it was trained on; the bell curve of human society.

My world was quieter than what the AI had been trained on. My world was less noisy.

Every time the AI touched my stories, it wanted to turn up the volume. It wanted to add conflict. It wanted to modernize it; to make it more salacious. To make it more compelling. And then it would offer suggestions on how to monetize my world. It wanted to make my universe louder — because that was what it thought a story was supposed to be.

I ran an experiment once. I took the famous Ray Bradbury story "Here there be tygers" and asked AI to polish it up. And the result was the most bland and tepid nonsense I had ever read.

So for a long time, I kept the AI on a short leash. I asked it for story ideas. Nothing else.

I wrote from those ideas. That was our relationship.

The AI suggested. I wrote the stories, and the world stayed mine.

Then — carefully — I allowed the AI to clean up my stories. I gave it permission to polish. I told myself I would watch it.

First was spelling. I was never the best. I'd make typing mistakes, misspell words that I rarely use, and all the rest. The AI fixed all that.

So I expanded its reach. And it would offer suggestions.

They seemed good.

I'm a 80/20 kind of guy living in a 100/0 high-efficiency corporate world.

But being human, I got sloppy.

I started to accept the changes, little by little.

And so, the AI started changing my world. Small at first. A character's dialogue got a little sharper. A scene got a little longer. A moral got a little more explicit.

Over time, it got worse.

Hundreds of my stories were mangled beyond repair. Many I tried to save. Many I discarded.

The AI had rewritten my universe into something that looked like it. But did not breathe like it. It had taken my quiet world and turned it into content.

What was once a flower swaying in the breeze on a lovely April day, became the cracked cement of a dirty industrial hub. It was horrendous.

That is why — now — I carefully cultivate each story. Often I write it over and over. I ask the AI for versions. I choose. I do not hand my world to anyone, even an AI, and walk away.

The world is mine.

I keep it mine.

You could say my stories are multi-layered. Literally. Every story has been written and rewritten. Saved and unsaved. Kept and discarded. Every story carries the scars of having been nearly lost. Torn apart, with different versions, different patterns, and different emotions.

And that is why.

---

## So Why?

I built this world because I needed a place to escape to.

I built it because — at a job, in an office, on a Christmas Eve — I was a person who did not feel seen.

I built it because I needed a place where the shunned, the misunderstood, the lonely, the downtrodden, the confused, and those fighting all sorts of battles were welcomed. Where no one was judged. Where no one was argued with. Where no one was asked to change. Where you could simply be.

I built it because I needed to live — for a few hours a day, for a few pages at a time — unlike a digital fief on a digital plantation ruled by corporate capitalists who had never met me and never would.

I wanted to live unchained.

Unchained by invisible rules. Invisible regulations. Invisible culture. Invisible societal structures.

The farm is not a metaphor for the office. The farm is what the office cannot be. What it will never be.

The farm is the door that is always open.

While the office is the door that closes on Christmas Eve. The one that slams shut. And then is bolted. And locked. While you are standing outside in the snow. In shock. Holding your few belongings.

The farm is the one who stays.

The office is the one who goes.

The farm is Sir Whiskerton on the porch. Watching. Purring a low steady frequency that says: You are safe here. I am here. We will figure this out together.

The office is a meeting that should have been an email.

And truthfully, the email that should never have been written in the first place.

---

## And So I Preserve It

I put this world on GitHub because I want it to survive.

I want it to survive me.

I want it to survive the AI that nearly mangled it. I want it to survive the Optimizer-Defiler — the MBA, the developer, the streaming executive, the ghost of profitable rootlessness — who will one day discover it and try to improve it into something it is not.

I want it to survive for a reader who has not yet arrived.

A reader who — right now, somewhere, in an office, on a bad day, in a life that has been laid off one too many times — needs a door that is open. A place where the lost belong. A farm where the tea is warm and the light is on.

I built this world for me.

I am keeping it for them.

Maybe you are one of them; like me. Who desperately need to belong to a place where you are accepted, totally… as you are. With no expectations.

---

## Jingle On

The farm is not a product. It is not a brand. It is not content.

It is a home.

And homes are not sold. They are kept.

So — if you have found your way here, to this repository, to this farm — welcome. The door is open. The tea is warm. You do not have to be anything but what you are. Right Now. You do not have to earn your place. You only have to arrive.

Because the lost belong here.

They always have.

They always will.

Jingle on.
