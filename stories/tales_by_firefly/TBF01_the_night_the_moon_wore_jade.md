# TBF01 — The Night the Moon Wore Jade

*A Sir Whiskerton Story*

> *A secret visit. A sleepless cat. A poem no one understands.*

---

## Part One: The Descent

The farm did not know she was there.

That was the point. That was always the point.

Long Yi descended through the cloud layer like a thought she had been holding for a hundred years — silent, slow, deliberate. Her wings did not beat so much as *persuade* the air to move aside. The moon, full and foolish, caught her shadow and painted it across the barn roof for exactly three seconds before she tucked her wings and landed in the tall grass beyond the fence.

She had never done this before.

She had told herself, for decades, that she never would.

And yet here she was.

Her claws sank into soft earth. Her lantern eyes scanned the farm like a scholar scanning a forbidden text. The barn, silvered by moonlight. The coop, quiet except for the occasional sleep-cluck of a hen. The pigsty, where something snored with the contentment of a creature who had never known worry. The tool shed, its window glowing faintly with the light of some experiment that was probably best left unexplained.

And the pond — where something yodeled faintly in its sleep. *Yodel-ay-hee-hoo*, it murmured. *Yodel-ay-hee-hoo.*

Long Yi blinked.

She had seen many strange things in her long life. She had seen mountains rise and rivers change course. She had seen dragons older than memory fold themselves into the shape of clouds and drift away. She had seen her parents change form and vanish into the space between heartbeats.

But she had never seen a pond that yodeled.

*This*, she thought, *is what he chose. This is where he comes. This is the place he calls home.*

She did not understand it.

She did not need to.

She moved forward, silent as starlight, and began to walk the perimeter of the farm.

---

She found the chicken coop first.

It was a modest structure — wooden, weathered, with a small door that had been repaired so many times it was more patch than plank. Long Yi peered through a crack in the wall and saw a cluster of hens huddled together on their roosts, their feathers fluffed against the night chill.

One of them — a brown hen with a particularly self-important expression, even in sleep — shifted and muttered something that sounded like *"I still say the fence was crooked."*

Long Yi tilted her head.

She had never understood the appeal of chickens. They were small. They were noisy. They were, in her considered opinion, *dramatic*. And yet her brother — her enormous, fire-breathing, mountain-born brother — had chosen to live among them.

*Why?* she wondered.

She moved on.

---

The pigsty was next.

A single pig lay on his back in the hay, his legs twitching in some dream, his snout pointed at the moon. He was smiling. He was *definitely* smiling. Long Yi watched him for a long moment, trying to understand what could possibly make a creature so content.

As if in answer, the pig muttered in his sleep: *"More dumplings... yes... perfect..."*

Long Yi's whiskers twitched.

*Dumplings*, she thought. *Of course.*

She had heard the stories. The winds had told her. Her brother had a weakness for dumplings — for "peasant food," as she called it, with the fond disdain of an older sister who had once watched him eat nine helpings of bamboo shoot stew and then complain of a bellyache for a week.

She did not smile.

She was a dragon of the Eastern Skies. She did not *smile*.

But something in her expression softened, just slightly, just for a moment.

She moved on.

---

The tool shed was the strangest.

Its window glowed with a faint, pulsing light — not candlelight, not firelight, but something else entirely. Something that hummed. Something that smelled of rosemary and regret and something that was almost, but not quite, burnt sugar.

Long Yi peered through the window.

Inside, a raccoon in a stained lab coat was hunched over a workbench, surrounded by bubbling beakers and scribbled notes and what appeared to be a jar of vibrating green jelly. He was muttering to himself.

"No, no, no — the viscosity is all wrong. The *emotional resonance* is there, but the texture... the *texture* is a tragedy. A culinary tragedy. Dr. Notorious, we must begin again."

A white lab rat on the corner of the bench looked up, twitched its nose, and went back to sleep.

Long Yi watched for a moment longer.

*This*, she thought, *is where the yodeling comes from. I am almost certain of it.*

She moved on.

---

And then she found him.

He was curled into a patch of moonlit clover, just beyond the fence line. His tail was wrapped around himself twice — an impressive feat, given his size — and his whiskers twitched at some dream she would never know. His scales shimmered in the silver light, old gold turned to new silver.

He was purring.

Not the deep, rumbling purr of a dragon. Not the throaty growl of a creature who could level mountains. But a soft, rhythmic, contented purr — the purr of a cat who has found the perfect sunbeam and has decided that the world can wait.

Long Yi stood at the edge of the clover and watched him.

She did not move.

She did not speak.

She simply stood there, enormous and still, her lantern eyes soft with something she could not name.

She remembered the first time she had seen him. Not as he was now — not as this gentle, impossible creature who had somehow convinced an entire farm that he was a cat — but as he had been. A hatchling. Small. Frightened. His scales still soft, his eyes still wide, his fire still more smoke than flame.

She remembered carrying him through the rubble of the Jade Valley. She remembered the weight of him in her claws. She remembered the way he had looked up at her, his tiny voice shaking, and asked: *"Where are they? Where did they go?"*

She had not had an answer then.

She did not have an answer now.

But she had learned, over the years, that some questions did not need answers. Some questions only needed *presence*. The presence of someone who would stay. Someone who would watch. Someone who would send a gentle wind when the world grew too cold.

*You*, she whispered to the wind, *are impossible.*

The wind did not answer. The wind knew better.

She watched him for a long time. She did not know how long. Time moved differently for dragons — slower, stranger, more like a river than an arrow. But eventually, the moon began its descent toward the horizon, and Long Yi knew she had to leave.

She took one step closer.

She lowered her enormous head, just slightly, just enough to breathe in the scent of clover and moonlight and her brother's quiet, contented purr.

*"He's fine,"* she murmured.

And then — because she was alone, because no one could hear, because the wind had always been her only confidant — she added:

*"He's home."*

She turned to go.

And that was when she heard the drum.

---

## Part Two: The Beatnik

Jazzpurr the Beatnik Cat was awake.

He was always awake at this hour. The small hours were his hours. The hours when the square world slept and the cool world stirred. He was perched on the woodpile behind the barn, bongo drum balanced on his knees, beret tilted at exactly the right angle of existential disinterest.

He had been working on a poem about the moon.

The moon was a cliché. He knew that. But the moon was also *there*, and Jazzpurr had learned long ago that the best poems were the ones that pretended to be about the moon while actually being about something else entirely.

He was halfway through a line about *"the silver ache of things we cannot name"* when he saw her.

A shadow. A *large* shadow. Moving across the far pasture with a silence that was not natural and not unnatural but something in between — something that belonged to a different category altogether.

Jazzpurr's paws went still on the drum.

He did not run. He did not hide. He was a beatnik. He was cool. And coolness, in his philosophy, meant that when the universe sent you a giant jade dragon in the middle of the night, you did not panic. You *observed*. You *appreciated*. And then, if you were very lucky, you wrote something weird about it.

He watched her approach the sleeping form of Longwei — the big softie, the honorary feline, the dragon who had somehow convinced the entire farm that he was a cat — and he watched her stop just short of him.

She did not wake him.

She did not touch him.

She simply stood there, enormous and still, her lantern eyes soft with something Jazzpurr could not name but immediately wanted to write a poem about.

He did not know what she was. He did not know *who* she was. But he understood the shape of the moment. He understood the ache of it. The beautiful, wordless, impossible thing of it.

*This*, he thought, *is the realest thing I have ever seen.*

He picked up his drum.

He did not play loudly. He played *softly*. A quiet, rolling rhythm — like rain on a roof, like a heartbeat heard through a wall, like something remembered from a dream. He played the kind of rhythm that said: *I see you. I don't understand you. I don't need to. But I see you.*

The dragon's ear twitched.

She did not look at him. But she tilted her head — slightly, almost imperceptibly — in his direction. As if acknowledging him. As if thanking him. As if saying: *You are the only one who knows I am here. Keep it that way.*

Jazzpurr nodded.

He did not stop playing.

He played as she turned away from her brother. He played as she spread her wings — silent, slow, deliberate. He played as she rose into the sky, a shadow against the stars, a secret the moon would keep.

And he played as she vanished into the clouds, leaving behind nothing but the memory of jade and the faint, lingering scent of mountains.

He played for another hour.

He did not sleep.

He did not want to.

He had a poem to write.

---

## Part Three: The Poem

The next morning, the farm woke to find Jazzpurr on the woodpile, fur slightly damp from the dew, beret askew, a half-finished poem clutched in one paw. He was asleep. He was smiling. He had never looked more content.

Doris the Hen found him first.

She had been on her way to the feed trough — because Doris was always on her way to the feed trough, and because Doris believed that the early hen got the corn — when she spotted the beatnik cat slumped against the woodpile, snoring softly, a piece of paper fluttering in the morning breeze.

Doris was not a cat person.

She was not a *Jazzpurr* person.

But she was, above all else, a hen who could not resist a piece of paper.

She pecked at it. She pulled it free. She unfolded it with her beak — a skill she had perfected over years of stealing notes from the farmer's pockets — and she read it aloud, because Doris read everything aloud, and because Doris was nosy in the way that only hens can be nosy.

> *The moon wore jade last night.*
> *I saw the shape of a goodbye*
> *that wasn't a goodbye.*
> *I saw the shape of a hello*
> *that had been waiting years to happen.*
> *I did not name it.*
> *I did not need to.*
> *Some things are only real*
> *when no one is watching.*
> *Jingle on, sweet ghost.*
> *Jingle on.*

The hens clucked in confusion.

"What does it *mean*?" demanded Gertrude the Goose, who had waddled over at the first sign of drama. "Is it about *me*? It's about me, isn't it? Everything is about me."

"It's not about you," said Doris.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's *good*."

Gertrude honked in outrage.

Porkchop the Pig, who had been listening from his sty, trotted over and peered at the poem. He read it twice. He read it three times. Then he looked toward the far pasture, where Longwei was already curled up in a sunbeam, purring like nothing had happened.

"Something happened," Porkchop said.

"What?" asked Doris.

"I don't know. But something."

Sir Whiskerton, who had been watching from the barn roof — because Sir Whiskerton was always watching, and because Sir Whiskerton had learned long ago that the best mysteries revealed themselves to those who waited — said nothing.

He simply looked at the poem.

He looked at Longwei.

He looked at the sky.

And he thought: *Something happened. Something beautiful. Something I will never fully understand.*

But he did not say it.

Some things, as Jazzpurr had written, are only real when no one is watching.

---

Jazzpurr woke an hour later.

He stretched. He yawned. He adjusted his beret. He looked around at the gathered animals — the hens, the goose, the pig, the cat on the roof — and he smiled a slow, beatnik smile.

"Read my poem, did you?" he asked.

"It doesn't make *sense*," Gertrude said.

"It's not supposed to."

"Then what's the *point*?"

Jazzpurr picked up his drum. He tapped out a quiet rhythm — the same rhythm he had played the night before, the same rhythm that had acknowledged the jade ghost and sent her on her way.

"The point," he said, "is that some things are real even when they don't make sense. The point is that some things are true even when no one can prove them. The point is that the moon wore jade last night, and I was there, and I saw it, and I wrote it down."

He tapped the drum again.

"The point," he said, "is that I don't need you to believe me. I just need you to *listen*."

The animals were silent.

And then, one by one, they drifted away — back to their feed, back to their chores, back to the ordinary business of being ordinary animals on an ordinary farm.

But Sir Whiskerton stayed.

He stayed on the barn roof, watching the sky, watching the pasture, watching the place where Longwei slept in his sunbeam.

And he thought: *I believe you, Jazzpurr. I believe you.*

---

## Part Four: The Return

Long Yi returned to the Jade Cloud Range before dawn.

She did not tell anyone where she had been. She did not need to. She simply settled onto her peak, wrapped her floral scarf a little tighter, and watched the sun rise over the mountains.

*"He's fine,"* she murmured to the wind.

The wind did not answer. The wind knew better.

But somewhere far below, in a cluttered toolshed that smelled of old books and burnt sugar, a half-crazed raccoon chef paused mid-experiment and frowned at the ceiling.

"I feel a poem," he said to Dr. Notorious.

Dr. Notorious, the white lab rat, did not respond. Dr. Notorious had heard this before.

"It's jade-colored," Remy added. "And it *yearns*."

He wrote it down. He forgot it immediately. But the poem — like all good poems, like all real things — stayed in the air of the farm, waiting for someone to notice it.

---

Long Yi sat on her peak for a long time.

She thought about the farm. She thought about the chickens, and the pig, and the yodeling pond. She thought about the raccoon with the glowing jelly, and the beatnik cat with the drum, and the way the moon had painted her shadow across the barn roof for exactly three seconds before she had tucked her wings and landed.

She thought about her brother.

She thought about the way he had purred in his sleep. The way his whiskers had twitched. The way he had looked — not like a dragon, not like a cat, but like something in between. Something that belonged to both worlds and neither.

*He's home*, she thought.

And then — because she was alone, because no one could hear, because the wind had always been her only confidant — she added:

*And I am glad.*

---

## Epilogue: The Full Moon

Jazzpurr never told anyone who the moon had worn that night.

But he never forgot, either.

And every full moon, without fail, he would climb onto the woodpile, pick up his drum, and play a quiet rhythm for a ghost who might or might not be watching.

Sometimes, he would see a shadow cross the moon.

Sometimes, he would hear a faint, distant sound — like wind through bamboo, like rain on a roof, like something remembered from a dream.

And sometimes — just sometimes — he would feel a presence settle onto the far pasture, silent and enormous and full of a love that had no name.

He would not look. He would not speak. He would simply play.

And the jade ghost would listen.

And the moon would wear jade.

And the farm would sleep, safe and warm and protected by a love it would never fully understand.

*Jingle on, sweet ghost.*

*Jingle on.*

---

**The End**

## Story Notes

**Characters:** Long Yi, Jazzpurr the Beatnik Cat, Longwei, Sir Whiskerton, Doris the Hen, Gertrude the Goose, Porkchop the Pig, Chef Remy LeRaccoon, Dr. Notorious

**Setting:** The farm, under a full moon

**Timeline:** Post-Longwei's integration into farm life; pre-any potential Long Yi on-farm appearance in canon

**Themes:** Distant love, secret guardianship, wordless understanding, the reality of things no one can prove

**In-Universe Artifacts Introduced:**
- Jazzpurr's poem, *"The Moon Wore Jade"*
- The recurring motif of the full moon as a night when the farm feels "a little more protected"
- The wordless bond between Jazzpurr and Long Yi

**Canon Notes:**
- This story establishes that Long Yi has visited the farm at least once, secretly, at night.
- No one knows but Jazzpurr (and possibly Sir Whiskerton, who suspects).
- The farm's "soft place" rule is honored: nothing dies; things change form. Long Yi's parents changed form and vanished from her perspective, not died.

**Future Story Seeds:**
- Sir Whiskerton asks Jazzpurr about the poem, and Jazzpurr gives him a cryptic, beatnik non-answer.
- Long Yi visits again — this time, she sees something that changes her understanding of the farm.
- Longwei discovers the poem and realizes, slowly, what it means.
- The full moon becomes a recurring motif — a night when the farm feels just a little more magical.
