# TBF04 — Esmeralda the Croc-a-Gator

*A Sir Whiskerton Story*

> *A lost croc-a-gator. A cat who does not ask questions. And a bowl of cream on a windowsill.*

---

## Part One: The Long Walk North

She had been walking for *three weeks*.

She did not know why she kept walking. She did not know why she had not turned back. She did not know what she was looking for, or where she was going, or what she would do when she got there.

What she knew was this: she had heard a *song*. And the song had come from *north*. And she had followed it.

She could no longer hear it, of course. The song had faded somewhere around the second river crossing, somewhere south of a place whose name she did not know. But she kept walking anyway, because *turning back* felt like something she could not do, and because the *walking itself* had become its own kind of song.

She was a *croc-a-gator*. She had been told this her whole life. *Half alligator, half crocodile, all trouble* — that was what Mémé had always said, with a laugh that was half growl and half lullaby.

*"You don' fit in the bayou, cher,"* Mémé had told her once. *"But you don' fit nowhere else, neither. So you make your own fit. That's the *croc-a-gator way*."*

Mémé had *changed form* the following spring. Esmeralda had been *alone* ever since.

She had walked through swamps and rivers and fields. She had walked through towns she did not enter and forests she did not stay in. She had eaten fish, and berries, and — once, memorably — a single, entire *chicken sandwich* that someone had left unattended at a picnic table. She had apologized to the sandwich. She had apologized to the person who had left it. She had apologized to the table.

She had not spoken to another creature in *nineteen days*.

And then, on a Tuesday, in the middle of an afternoon rainstorm, she arrived at the edge of a farm.

She did not know it was a farm. She did not know anything about it. She knew only that it *smelled right* — like wet earth and warm animals and *old wood* — and that she was *too tired to walk another step*, and that there was a *shadow behind an old tractor* that looked *exactly large enough* for her.

She curled into the shadow. She closed her eyes. She told herself she would stay for *one night*, and then she would *keep walking*, and then she would *find the song again*.

She stayed for three days.

---

## Part Two: The Cat Who Watched

Catnip noticed her on the *first night*.

He did not *see* her — not at first. He *felt* her. A change in the rhythm of the farm, a *weight* that had not been there before. He was sitting on the roof of the tool shed, doing what he always did at night: *watching*. Cataloguing. Making sure nothing about the farm had *changed* without his permission.

And then something *had* changed.

He crept toward the old tractor. He kept to the shadows. He moved the way he had moved his whole life — the way a cat moves when the world has taught him that every shadow might be a trap.

What he found was *not a trap*. It was a *croc-a-gator*, curled into the shadow behind the tractor, breathing slowly, *shivering* slightly in the cold night air. She was *enormous*. She was *out of place*. She was *terrified* — he could see that at once. The way she held her claws tucked in. The way she kept her head down. The way she did not move, even when she *heard* him, even when she *knew* he was there, even when she must have *sensed* that she was *seen*.

She was not *hiding*. She was *waiting*. Waiting for someone to *decide what to do*.

Catnip sat down.

He did not say anything. He did not *need* to say anything. He simply *sat*. And after a long while, the croc-a-gator — whose eyes he could now see in the moonlight, dark-honey and *watchful* — opened her mouth and whispered something that sounded, to his ears, like a *prayer*.

*"Cher,"* she said. *"I don' mean no trouble, me. I jus'... I jus' got lost."*

Catnip looked at her for a long moment.

*"You're not lost,"* he said. *"You're just late."*

And then he walked away. He did not *invite* her in. He did not *offer* her anything. He simply *walked away*, the way a cat does when he wants you to understand that he will be *back*.

He came back, an hour later, with *three fish*.

He dropped them at the edge of the shadow. He did not *look* at her. He did not *wait*. He simply *left*, the way a cat leaves when he wants you to understand that he does not need to be *thanked*.

She ate the fish. She wept, quietly, into the shadow. She had never been *fed* by anyone since Mémé had changed form.

## Part Three: The Second Bowl

Martha noticed the *second bowl* within a week.

She had not *seen* the croc-a-gator. She had not *looked* for her. But she *knew*. Martha always knew. The way she knew when a hen was sick before the hen knew. The way she knew when the Farmer had forgotten to eat. The way she knew when Catnip had stayed out all night — and *why*.

She did not say anything.

She simply started leaving *two bowls* on the windowsill instead of one. She did not *label* them. She did not *explain* them. She simply *left them*, and *refilled them*, and *left them again*.

She also started humming *a little louder* in the evenings, when she did the dishes. A soft, low, *uncomplicated* hum. The kind of hum that says: *I am here. You are welcome. You do not need to explain.*

Catnip noticed. Catnip *always* noticed. And for the first time in his life, he did not know what to *do* with what he noticed.

He stood on the porch one evening, watching Martha hang the wash, watching the second bowl *disappear* in the dark. And he felt something in his chest that he did not have a name for. Something that was *not* strategy. Something that was *not* empire. Something that was, in fact, *the opposite of both*.

He turned and walked back to the tractor. He sat down beside the croc-a-gator. He said nothing.

*"Cher,"* she whispered, after a long while. *"Who left the cream?"*

*"The woman,"* Catnip said. *"Martha."*

*"She know 'bout me?"*

*"Yes."*

The croc-a-gator was quiet for a long moment.

*"She mad?"*

*"No,"* Catnip said. *"She's not mad."*

The croc-a-gator — *Esmeralda*, though he did not yet know her name — began to cry. Quietly. The way a creature cries who has spent a very long time *expecting to be sent away*.

Catnip did not *comfort* her. He did not *touch* her. He simply *stayed*, the way a cat stays when he wants you to understand that he is *not going anywhere*.

---

## Part Four: The Question of Names

She told him her name on the *ninth night*.

*"Esmeralda,"* she said. *"Esmeralda Thibodeaux. My Mémé, she name me after a song she love."*

*"Esmeralda,"* Catnip repeated. He turned the name over in his mouth the way a cat turns over a new smell. *"That's a lot of name."*

*"Mais, yeah,"* she said, and laughed — a soft, low, rumbling laugh that was *almost* a purr. *"Mémé, she always say I got too much name for a croc-a-gator. She say I need to *grow into it*."*

*"You did,"* Catnip said.

Esmeralda was quiet for a long moment.

*"Cher,"* she said finally. *"Why you help me?"*

Catnip did not answer right away. He had been *asking himself* the same question for nine days. He did not have an *answer* that made sense in any of the ledgers he had spent his life keeping.

He had only *a feeling*. A feeling that had *nothing to do* with strategy, and *everything to do* with a small, guarded truth he had never quite admitted to himself.

*"Because someone helped me once,"* he said. *"A long time ago. When I was *lost*, and I did not know it."*

Esmeralda looked at him. Her dark-honey eyes were *warm* and *watchful* and *wondering*.

*"I thank you, cher,"* she said.

*"You don't have to thank me,"* Catnip said.

*"I know,"* she said. *"That's why I do."*

---

## Part Five: The Coming Out

It happened on a *Sunday*.

The Farmer had gone to town. The animals were all *busy* — Doris clucking about the state of the coop, Porkchop wallowing in the mud, the hens gossiping about nothing at all. Sir Whiskerton was on the roof. Catnip was asleep in his usual spot, curled into a tight black comma against the warm wood of the porch.

And Esmeralda — *tired* of hiding, *tired* of the shadow, *tired* of being *almost seen* — walked *out into the sun*.

She did not *announce* herself. She did not *ask permission*. She simply *stood*, enormous and silver-green and *utterly out of place*, in the middle of the barnyard.

The farm went *silent*.

Doris stopped mid-cluck. Porkchop stopped mid-wallop. The hens stopped mid-gossip. Even the bees, for one uncanny moment, seemed to *stop buzzing*.

And then — from the roof — Sir Whiskerton said, *in a voice that carried across the entire farm*:

*"Ah. The new neighbor."*

The farm *gasped*.

Catnip *woke up* — suddenly, sharply, the way a cat wakes when he has been *tricked into sleeping* — and *launched* himself off the porch, *sprinting* toward Esmeralda with a look of *sheer terror* on his face.

He got there just in time to *stand between her and the farm*, his fur puffed out, his tail lashing, *ready to fight*.

*"She's with me,"* he said. *"Anyone who has a problem can take it up with me."*

The farm *stared*.

And then *Martha* — who had come to the screen door to see what all the fuss was about — *laughed*.

She laughed a soft, warm, *uncomplicated* laugh. The kind of laugh that says: *You are all being very silly, and I love you all very much.*

She pushed the screen door open. She walked down the porch steps. She walked right past Catnip — right past his *bristling fury* — and she stopped in front of Esmeralda.

She looked up. And up. And up.

*"Well,"* she said. *"You're a big one."*

Esmeralda — who had been *preparing to be sent away* — burst into tears.

*"Cher,"* she sobbed. *"I don' mean no trouble, me. I jus' got lost, and I ain' got nowhere to go, and I ain' got nobody, and I—"*

Martha held up a hand.

*"Hush, honey,"* she said. *"You're not going anywhere. There's a second bowl on the windowsill, and I made a pot of rice and gravy, and if you don't come inside and eat it, I'll be very disappointed."*

Esmeralda stared.

*"...Rice and gravy?"* she whispered.

*"And cream,"* Martha said. *"Every evening, right on the sill. For as long as you want to stay."*

Esmeralda looked at Catnip. Catnip looked at Esmeralda. Catnip looked at Martha. And for the first time in his *entire life*, he did not know what to say.

Sir Whiskerton — still on the roof — cleared his throat.

*"I believe,"* he said, *"the correct response is 'thank you, Martha.'"*

Catnip opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

*"...Thank you, Martha,"* he said.

Martha smiled. She turned back toward the house. She paused at the door.

*"You know,"* she said, over her shoulder, *"you didn't have to hide her. You could have just asked."*

Catnip blinked.

*"...I didn't know if you'd say yes."*

Martha looked at him for a long moment. And then — *softly* — she said:

*"You never had to ask."*

And she went inside to warm the gravy.

---

## Epilogue: The Second Bowl

Esmeralda stayed.

She did not stay *in the shadow*. She stayed *on the porch*, in the sun, next to Catnip, next to Martha, next to the second bowl of cream that had been left for her before she ever *knew* she needed it.

She learned the farm's routines. She learned the names of every hen and every pig and every cat. She learned that Sir Whiskerton did *not* like being called *"a nice kitty."* She learned that Porkchop would *eat anything*, including — once, memorably — the *beaded necklace* she had been wearing at the time. She learned that *every creature on the farm* had, at some point, been *lost* in some way, and that every one of them had been *found* by someone else.

She learned the farm. And the farm learned *her*.

She sang Cajun lullabies to the hens when they were frightened. She told the piglets *stories* about the bayou — about Mémé, about the *loup-garou*, about the *feu follet* that danced in the swamp at night. She taught the Farmer to say *"cher"* when he was talking to Martha, and the Farmer — who *did not know why he was doing it* — did it anyway.

And every evening, she sat on the porch with Catnip, watching the sun go down, humming softly, eating rice and gravy, drinking cream.

*"Cher,"* she said one evening.

*"Hm?"* Catnip said.

*"You know what I love 'bout this place?"*

*"No."*

*"It don' matter what I am. Ain' nobody here ever ask me what I am. They jus' ask if I want more cream."*

Catnip was quiet for a long moment.

*"That's the farm,"* he said. *"That's what it does."*

Esmeralda reached over — slowly, carefully, the way she did everything — and nudged his shoulder with her enormous, scaly head.

*"I know, cher,"* she said. *"I know."*

And she did.

## Moral

*Home is not a place. Home is a recognition. You do not have to know where you are going. You only have to know when you have arrived.*

## Starring

- **Esmeralda the Croc-a-Gator** (沼心鳄) — the *lost soul from the bayou*, who finds a home not by *earning* it, but by *recognizing* it when it is offered.
- **Catnip the Stray Cat** (坏蛋猫) — the *sovereign shadow*, who protects something *for the first time in his life* without asking anything in return. This is one of the most *important* stories in his arc.
- **Martha** — the *quiet heart of the farm*, who knew *before anyone told her*, and who *waited* without ever asking a question.
- **Sir Whiskerton** (胡子爵士) — the *witness*, who knew *before even Catnip did*, and who delivered the *one perfect line* that broke the tension.
- **The Farm Animals** — Doris, Porkchop, the hens, and the rest — who *gasped* at first, and then *moved on*, the way the farm always does.
- **The Second Bowl** — the *artifact of the story*. The bowl that was left, before it was needed. The proof that *someone was waiting*.
