# Author's Notes: Collection 2

*Behind the stories of The Symphony of the Soil.*

## A Note Before We Begin

Collection 1's Author's Notes were about *how the stories came to be*.

Collection 2's Author's Notes are about something *harder*. They are about *why the stories had to be written*.

Most of the stories in this collection were written during a period of personal chaos. I was between jobs — *repeatedly*, in a world that had stopped treating professionals as anything other than disposable. I was watching a career I had trained for dissolve, and rebuilding something *quieter* in its place. And in the middle of all of that, I was writing about a farm.

The farm was not a *distraction*. It was a *life raft*. And these notes are the *backstories* — the small, true, sometimes painful things that *prompted* each story. They are not *explanations*. They are *origins*.

I've written them in the first person, because they are mine. They are not meant to be literature. They are meant to be *the truth behind the fable*. Some are *grand*. Some are *mundane*. Both kinds *matter*.

— Robert

## Story 2A003 — Sir Whiskerton and the Great Toilet Paper Shortage

This story was written during COVID.

I was in China. The pandemic had emptied the streets, closed the schools, and — for a few strange months — made the entire world *very, very quiet*. And I was watching, from a continent away, as my home country went to war over *toilet paper*.

Not rice. Not water. Not canned goods. Not medicine. **Toilet paper.**

I remember thinking: *this is the funniest thing I have ever seen*. And also: *this is the most revealing thing I have ever seen*.

Because the panic wasn't about *survival*. The panic was about *disposable conveniences treated as necessities*. It was about a culture that had decided, at some point, that the *softest* thing in the bathroom was *the most essential thing in the house*. And the minute that softness disappeared, the whole society *fell apart*.

The story came out of that. Nutters didn't create the panic. He *noticed* it, and *minted* it into coin. He is *not the villain*. He is *the market*. And the moral — *"the sun on your back, the food in your soil, the steady hearts beside you"* — is what I was trying to remember while watching my own culture *lose its mind over two-ply paper*.

I still find it funny. I probably always will.

— Robert

## Story 2A004 — Sir Whiskerton and the Case of Handy Hank and the Perplexing Plumbing

I want to tell you the real story behind this one.

I was laid off once. And then again. And then again. *Seven times*, in fact, and — because the universe has a very specific sense of humor — *more than one of those layoffs arrived on Christmas Eve*.

I am the firstborn son of a father who believed in a world that no longer exists. In his world, you went to good schools. You got a good job. You gave that job your loyalty — your *life* — and the job gave you *a life* in return. A pension. A place. A story you could tell at the end of it. My father was a professional. He was treated like a professional. And he assumed, reasonably, that his son would be too.

I went to the schools. I got the jobs. I earned the accolades. And I was laid off anyway — again, and again, and again — because the world I actually entered treats professionals not as *people* but as *inventory*. Toilet paper. Styrofoam coffee cups. Used lighters. Things you hold for a moment and throw away when the quarter turns.

My father did not understand. How could he? In his world, the contract was sacred. In my world, the contract was *temporary* — and *nobody told the fathers*.

He would look at me with great sadness in his eyes and ask, in the softest, most helpless voice I have ever heard from a man, *"Why can't you keep and hold a job?"*

He was not blaming me. He was *grieving*. He was watching a version of his son — the *promising* version, the *firstborn* version, the version he had built a hundred quiet hopes around — dissolve into a stranger who *kept losing things* through no fault of his own. He was watching a promise he had made to his own father — *the next generation will do better* — come apart in a way that neither of us had language for.

I do not hold this against him. I hold it *with* him.

That is why Hank exists.

Hank is the man who was told, over and over, in a thousand quiet ways, *"Good for nothing."* Hank is the man who tried to be *strong enough* to fix what could not be fixed by strength. Hank is the man who mistook *force* for *value*, because *force* was the only thing the world seemed to reward. And Hank is the man who finally, in a barn full of water, learned the one thing that the world he was born into *never taught him*:

*The system is not your enemy. The system is just a system. And the way to survive in a system is not to overpower it. It is to understand it. To find the valve. To turn the water off.*

Hank does not stop being Hank. He does not stop carrying Old Persuader. He does not stop using a sledgehammer for a thumbtack. But he *pauses* now, before he swings. And that pause — that small, unheroic, unapplauded pause — is the quiet dignity of a man who *keeps trying in a world designed to discard him*.

Somewhere, a father is watching. He is not saying anything. He is just... watching.

And the watching *is* the blessing.

— Robert

## Story 2A007 — The Cluckaneer's Code

This story was inspired by **Johnny Depp** as **Captain Jack Sparrow** in *Pirates of the Caribbean*.

I was, at the time, in a phase where I was experimenting with AI-generated art. And the *thing* I was generating most often was *pirates in tropical seas*. Big ships. Blue water. Palm trees. Rum. All of it. The aesthetic was *obsessive*, and I couldn't stop making them.

But when I sat down to write a *pirate* story for the farm, I hit a wall. **You cannot put a tropical pirate in upstate New York.** The landscape *refuses it*. The mood *refuses it*. The farm is *not* the Caribbean, and a pirate in a snowstorm is *absurd* in the wrong way.

So I looked for a substitute. I thought about *birds*. Which birds could conceivably *be* a pirate? Chickens. Parrots. Ducks. Hawks. Tucans. And I chose *chicken*, because — for whatever reason — that is the one that *clicked*. A rooster with an eyepatch and a tricorn hat, commanding a picnic-table ship on a duck pond.

The other ingredient was a *seriously power-hungry supervisor at work*. Someone who wanted to *run everything*, who insisted on *rules for the sake of rules*, who could not understand *why anyone would do anything other than what they were told*. That person became *Cluckbeard's crisis*. Not the *villain* of the story, but the *wound* at its center.

And so the story came out: a *delusional bird*, trying to *build a nation* on a *picnic table*, learning that a *constitution* is only as strong as its citizens' willingness to *reinterpret it*.

The tropical pirates never made it into the farm. But *one* of them — *translated into feathers and a duck pond* — did.

— Robert

## Story 2B001 — Disco Dazzle and Zephyr's Cosmic Dance Card

I lived through the disco period. I was attending university. It was quite the time.

I had forgotten all about that era until the television show *That '70s Show* appeared, and suddenly I remembered my **AMC Pacer** — *yellow*, with **orange shag carpeting** on the interior, an **8-track player** with an FM adapter, and a sound system that I played *much* too loud. I had the **platform shoes**. The **polyester open-collar shirt with the flared arms**. The **choker collar** around my neck. I jammed to the Bee Gees. To Earth, Wind & Fire. To the Commodores.

And then, one day, I was sitting in my office and I watched *two dogs playing in the yard outside*. And the *entire thing* — the Pacer, the shag, the choker, the Bee Gees, the two dogs — *coalesced* into Disco Dazzle.

He is not a *character*. He is *an era*, wearing a dog's body.

And the *book*, oddly, is not about disco. It is about *belonging* — about the fact that *joy is not something you earn, it is something you join*, and that *the floor is always yours if you are willing to step onto it*.

Dazzle is the *era I lived*. The *story* is the *thing I learned from it*.

— Robert

## Story 2B002 — Sir Whiskerton and the Cartography of Quiet Hearts

In the early 1980s, I was living with a girlfriend in **Fort Pierce, Florida**. She wanted a bird. We got a **cockatiel**.

Not a parakeet. A cockatiel. And I learned *so much* from that bird.

The first thing I learned was that it was *social*. When my girlfriend went away for two months, the bird got *lonely*. And so I started taking his cage into the living room, opening the door, and letting him *hang out* with me. He would follow me around like a dog. And when I came to feed him, he would *sing* to me.

The second thing I learned was that it was *smart*. People use the term *"bird brain"* as an insult. It is not. This bird was *remarkably friendly, intelligent, and perceptive* — and with that in mind, and my thoughts on *relationships*, I generated this story.

Angus and Lucile are *the two halves of that lesson*. He is the *armadillo who cannot stop running*. She is the *parakeet who will not stop watching*. And the story is the moment they *finally stop orbiting each other* and *stand together and look at the same fragile thing*.

The *"Here There Be Us"* on the margin of their shared map is *the thing that cockatiel taught me*: that attention is a form of company. That a small, patient, intelligent creature will *choose to be near you* — if you *let it*.

— Robert

## Story 2B003 — Sir Whiskerton and Ferdinand's Fowl Fortune Teller

Years ago, I started noticing something.

I was *making things*. Writing. Building. Living. And I started to notice that the things I was making had a *soundtrack* — a *rhythm*, a *composed order of events*, that made the moments of my life feel *authored*, *on purpose*, *held*.

And then I realized something *harder*.

I was not the author.

The moments were *already happening*. The composing I was doing was not *writing the score* — it was *listening to it*. The best thing I could do was not to *impose* my melody on the world. It was to *accompany* it. Match its tempo. Play along.

That is what Ferdinand learns in this story.

It is also, quietly, what I have learned in *my* life.

— Robert

## Story 2B006 — Martha and the Night Watch

There is a woman in this story. She is not Martha. She is the woman Martha *is modeled on* — a woman of quiet strength, of practical care, of *staying*. And there is a cat in this story. He is not Catnip. He is a cat I have known — *frightened, guarded, and yet longing for warmth*.

The storm is *every storm*. The thunder is *every thunder*. And the hum — the low, D-minor hum that Martha holds against the fear — is *the sound my mother used to make* when I was a child and the world was loud.

I wrote this story for her. And for every woman who has *sat with a frightened creature* and *not tried to fix it*, but simply *held it until the echo found a softer place to land*.

— Robert

## Story 2C003 — The Great Asparagus Catastrophe

The asparagus bed in this story is real.

When my mother had cancer, she needed to *get out and about*. She needed something to *do*, something to *watch grow*, something that would *remind her that the world was still turning*. So I built her some planters — *1.5-meter-square boxes made from railroad ties*, filled with soil. And she planted asparagus.

Lordy. Did they *grow*.

They *kept growing*. Year after year. We had *enough asparagus to feed an army*. To this day, I suspect, they are still growing. Who would think that such a common, ordinary vegetable would be *so flourishing*? Not me. And not her. But she was *delighted*.

She also had flower planters — annuals and perennials — and a small vegetable garden. I took care of all of it when I lived with her. The garden was a *magnet for rabbits*, and I could not keep them out *no matter how hard I tried*. In retrospect, I think they *enjoyed* the challenge.

The *Asparagites* in this story — the *sentient asparagus collective* — are *my mother's asparagus*, given a *voice*. They are demanding *recognition*. They are demanding *to be seen*. And they are demanding *to move with meaning*.

Because that is what she did. She *made something grow*, in a time of her life when *everything else was being taken away*. And the *asparagus responded* — with a flourishing that *no one could have predicted*.

That is the *origin* of the Asparagites. It is also the *reason they belong*.

— Robert

## Story 2C004 — Sir Whiskerton and the Great Farm Olympics

There was a Winter Olympics, hosted by China.

And there was a *dance* at the opening ceremony — hundreds of children, in white skates, moving in *loose clusters* across the ice, forming shapes, and then — at the end — *settling into a single enormous heart*.

And there was *one child*, standing *alone* at the *edge of the ice*. She had been *left out* of the formation. And one of the other children — *without any cue, without any instruction, without any visible decision* — *skated back*, took her hand, and *led her into the heart*.

It was *not planned*. It was *not scripted*. And I *still get emotional* when I remember it.

That is what this story is. The farm's Olympiad is *not about winning*. It is *not about the events*. It is about *the child who skates back*. It is about *the one who breaks ranks to bring someone else in*.

Sir Whiskerton *could* have won the whole contest by *playing the game*. He didn't. He *reframed the game entirely*. Because the game — the *real* game — is *the one where nobody gets left out*.

And that is the *Olympic spirit*, in the end. Not the medals. Not the records. The *one child, who skated back*.

— Robert

## A Final Note

Most of these stories were written during a period of personal chaos.

I was between jobs — repeatedly. I was watching a career I had trained for dissolve. I was dealing with pressures I could not always name, on timelines that were not my own, in systems that did not always work.

The farm was my escape. It was the place I went when the world outside was too loud. It was the place I *built* — one story at a time, one character at a time, one small moral at a time — because I *needed* a place to go.

Collection 1 was about *belonging*. Collection 2 is about *meaning*. And the *difference between them* is the *difference between finding a home and building a life in it*. Which is, I think, the *whole arc* of a person's life.

I made many mistakes along the way. There are typos. There are sentences that run too long. There are characters who took *three drafts* to become themselves. The mess is part of the work. It is *evidence* of the work — of a real person, in real life, doing something that mattered to them.

The farm is not a franchise. It is not a brand. It is a *place*, and I built it in the cracks of a busy life, because I needed somewhere to *be*.

If you are reading these notes, you have probably already read the stories. Thank you for caring about the *why* behind the *what*. Thank you for stepping into this small, true, silliest corner of my life.

I hope it feels like home to you.

— Robert
