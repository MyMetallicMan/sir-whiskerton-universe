# Sir Whiskerton and the Case of the Sibling Showdown

*A Tale of Two Thrones, One Father, and the Bagpipes in Their Blood*

*From the Collection 2 archive — Story 2B193*

---

Ah, dear reader. Today's tale is not about rivalry. It is about *inheritance*. About the different melodies two souls can take from the same sad, beautiful song. It is the story of two brothers, one stone wall, and the ghost of a bagpipe that taught them both what it means to be noble, in tragically opposite ways.

## I. The Arrival of the Specter

The farm felt the change *before* it saw it. The air, usually humming with the relaxed key of G-major contentment, tightened. It developed a theatrical crispness, as if waiting for a spotlight to click on.

Sir Whiskerton, from his roof, felt it in his whiskers. It was a familiar pressure. A specific, grandiose frequency. It wasn't Catnip's scheming hum or Bigcat's aggressive thrum. This was the sound of *velvet drapes being drawn*.

Then came the **Fanfare of One**. Not a carriage, but a rickety wheelbarrow piled with a faded tapestry and a tasseled pillow, pushed by a very confused field mouse. Standing atop it, one paw raised in a regal salute, was a cat in a moth-eaten velvet cape. His monocle was a washer from the Farmer's toolbox. His crown was a braided circle of oat stalks. He was, in every sense, a *kingdom of scraps*.

**Sir Cattenon.** The *Arrogant Meow-Knight.*

He didn't enter the farm. He *staged* his arrival.

"Behold!" he declared to a barnyard of bewildered chickens. "Your salvation has arrived on the wings of... *modest local transport*! Rejoice!"

Whiskerton descended, not with alarm, but with a deep, weary fondness. "Cattenon. The wheelbarrow. Isn't that a bit... *literal*?"

"Symbolism, dear brother!" Cattenon scoffed, leaping down with a flourish. "The common folk bearing their noble master! It's *classic*! Now, where is my throne? I assume you've kept the east-facing sunbeam polished?"

## II. The Two Interpretations of a Drone

Cattenon's "takeover" was not a coup. It was a *redesign*. He saw the farm not as a community, but as a *set*. The animals weren't comrades; they were a *cast*. The Farmer wasn't a steward; he was a *stagehand*.

He convened the animals. "My loyal subjects—"

"We're not subjects," rumbled Porkchop.

"*Future* loyal subjects!" Cattenon corrected without missing a beat. "I bring you a new doctrine: **The Doctrine of Conspicuous Leisure**! Under my rule, work shall be *optional*! Nay, *frowned upon*! Your only duty will be to look *picturesque* and attend my daily parades!"

The animals were baffled. But some, like Doris, were intrigued by the vocabulary. "*Conspicuous*... it does sound important."

Whiskerton watched, his tail-tip twitching. He saw not a tyrant, but a *terrified artist*. Cattenon was trying to *direct a play* to avoid being an *actor* in the messy, unscripted drama of real life.

The confrontation came at the old stone wall—*their father's wall.*

"You're disrupting the covenant, Cattenon," Whiskerton said, his voice low. "This farm runs on reciprocal care, not curated tableaus."

"Care? *Care?*" Cattenon's performative mask slipped for a second, revealing a raw, jagged edge. "Father cared. He cared so deeply he chased glory right off this wall and into the dark! I'm not disrupting anything. I'm... *improving the narrative*!"

There it was. The *wound*. To Cattenon, their father's disappearance wasn't a loss; it was a *plot hole*. And he was determined to write a *better, grander, safer* ending for himself.

## III. The Performance Cracks

Cattenon's reign of style immediately collided with substance.

He declared the mud patch a "royal spa." Porkchop, the master of the mud, simply oinked and rolled, covering the "Duke of Delusion" in glorious, unscripted filth. He tried to lead a "Triumphant Procession" through the garden, only to be dive-bombed by Beatrice's bees, who had no respect for royal airspace. His "Decree of Universal Nap Time" failed when the chickens, creatures of rigid routine, kept clucking on schedule, ruining the soundscape of serene silence he'd envisioned.

The final blow came from **Catnip**. The stray saw not a noble, but a *mark*.

"Your Grace," Catnip purred, oozing up to him. "A ruler of your stature needs an... *enforcer*. A keeper of the darker covenants. For, say, thirty percent of the cream tribute, I can ensure your parades are... uninterrupted."

Cattenon, for the first time, looked truly *afraid*. He didn't want a *sinister partner*. He wanted an *adoring audience*. He wanted to play the *noble king*, not make deals in the shadows. Catnip's offer revealed the dark truth behind his fantasy: that real power requires getting your paws dirty, and his were only clean because they never did any real work.

## IV. The Duel of the Drone

Whiskerton found him that evening, back on the old stone wall. The cape was muddy, the oat-stalk crown bent. Cattenon wasn't *posing*. He was just *sitting*, small, staring at the spot where their father's buzzing purr used to anchor the world.

"You don't understand," Cattenon whispered, the arrogance gone, leaving only a young, lost cat. "He was so... *big*. So sure. The bagpipes... they made everything make *sense*. Now it's just *quiet*. And in the quiet, I feel... *small*."

Whiskerton leapt up beside him. Not as a rival, but as a *brother*.

"I hear them too," Whiskerton said quietly.

"You do?"

"Every day. Not the loud part you try to play. The *drone*. The steady, endless note underneath. The note that says, *'I am here. I hold the line.'* Father didn't leave to chase glory, Cattenon. He left because he heard a *threat* to his wall. His *duty*. That was his melody."

Whiskerton looked at the farm. "My farm is my wall. The animals, the routines, the quiet repairs—that's my drone. You... you keep trying to play the *solo*. But a solo without a drone is just a *noise in the air*. It doesn't hold anything up."

Cattenon was silent. The truth wasn't that he was *wrong*. It was that he was playing the *wrong part of the song*.

## V. The New Harmony

Cattenon didn't leave in disgrace. He left with a *new assignment*.

He approached the Valley Chicks. "Your... productions," he said, his voice less booming. "They lack *narrative cohesion*! A *director* is needed!" He didn't take over; he offered his one true skill: a sense of *spectacle*. He helped them stage *"The Ballad of the Glistening Wonder: A Retrospective,"* and it was, admittedly, the most beautifully blocked and tragically lit show the henhouse had ever seen.

He told Whiskerton, "Your wall is... *admirable*. Dull, but admirable. I shall find my *own* wall. One that needs... *flourish*." He gave a small, real smile. "Tell the Farmer his wheelbarrow is by the gate. And his washer makes a *terrible* monocle."

The Moral of the Story, dear reader, is this: *Family is the first song we ever hear. Some of us spend our lives trying to replay the melody, loud and proud, afraid the world will forget the tune. Others learn to hum the drone, the steady, humble note that keeps the sky from falling. Neither is wrong. But the most beautiful music happens when we stop trying to drown each other out, and realize we're just playing different parts of the same heartbreaking, glorious song our father taught us before he walked into the dark, his bagpipes fading on the wind.*

Sir Cattenon, the Arrogant Meow-Knight, wheeled his barrow of dreams down the road. He was still a king. But now, perhaps, a king in search of a kingdom that needed a little less governing, and a *lot* more style.

And on the stone wall, Sir Whiskerton watched him go, hearing in his brother's departing, chattering instructions to the field mouse, a faint, familiar, and finally *understood* echo of the bagpipes they both would carry forever.

Jingle on. (Or, in the language of family: *Same song... different part... listen... remember.*)

The End.
