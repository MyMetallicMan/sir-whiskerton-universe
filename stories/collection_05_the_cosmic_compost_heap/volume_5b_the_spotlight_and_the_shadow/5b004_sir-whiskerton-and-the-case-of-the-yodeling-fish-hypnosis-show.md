# Sir Whiskerton and the Case of the Yodeling Fish Hypnosis Show

*Previously cataloged as 5B142*

## Foreword

Ah, dear reader, settle into the hayloft of your heart. Let the screen door sigh shut behind you. The air today smells of pond algae, polished wood, and the faint, rhythmic *yodel-ay-hee-hoo* of a trout that has discovered its inner Broadway star. 

Do not be alarmed. This is simply Tuesday on the farm: the day an eccentric pig tried to make aquatic stars out of bottom-feeders, the barnyard fell into a synchronized trance, and Sir Whiskerton reminded everyone—with one velvet paw on a dusty kazoo—that sometimes, the best way to break a spell is to be entirely, unapologetically annoying.

## I. The Yodeling Fish Take the Stage

It all began on a sunny, golden afternoon when Mr. Wigglesworth, the farm’s resident eccentric pig, announced his latest grand plan. 

Mr. Wigglesworth was a pig of refined, if slightly unhinged, tastes. He wore a tiny, slightly crushed top hat, and he carried a stalk of celery like a maestro’s baton. He stood atop a hay bale, addressing the gathered animals with theatrical gravity.

“Ladies and gentlemen, creatures of all species!” Mr. Wigglesworth declared, his voice booming. “I present to you… the Yodeling Fish Hypnosis Show!”

The animals gathered around the pond, curious but deeply skeptical. The Yodeling Fish—a trio of highly synchronized, deeply peculiar trout—were known for their habit of singing alpine folk songs while doing the backstroke. 

“I’ve built them a stage,” Mr. Wigglesworth continued, gesturing grandly to a rickety wooden platform he had constructed over the water, complete with a curtain made of woven reeds. “And tonight, they will perform for the entire farm! This is their big break. Their moment in the spotlight!”

Sir Whiskerton, who had been napping on the barn roof, opened one green eye. He adjusted his monocle, his tail flicking once in profound, weary anticipation. “This,” he muttered to Ditto, his ever-eager apprentice, “cannot possibly end well.”

Ditto tilted his head, his silver fur crackling with static excitement. “But what if the fish are really good? What if they become famous?”

“Fame is overrated, little echo,” Sir Whiskerton replied smoothly. “It usually comes with a terrible soundtrack. But I suppose we’ll find out soon enough.”

## II. The Hypnotic Performance

That evening, the farm animals gathered by the pond. Mr. Wigglesworth, wearing his top hat and a bow tie made of spinach leaves, introduced the fish with great fanfare. 

“Ladies and gentlemen, prepare to be amazed! Prepare to be enchanted! Prepare to be… yodeled at!”

The fish swam onto the stage, their scales glinting in the moonlight. They opened their mouths and began their performance. 

*“YODEL-AY-HEE-HOO!”*

At first, the animals were merely amused. Doris the Hen clucked, “This is highly irregular.” Rufus the Dog wagged his tail, his chartreuse glow pulsing at a happy 60 BPM. Porkchop the Pig munched happily on a bucket of popcorn.

But as the performance continued, the yodeling shifted. It became deeper, more resonant, vibrating with a strange, aquatic magic. The rhythm locked into a hypnotic, swirling waltz. 

Something strange happened. The animals began to sway in unison. Their eyes glazed over. 

Doris stopped clucking and started yodeling in perfect pitch. Rufus stopped wagging and began to waltz with an imaginary partner, his paws moving in flawless, sweeping arcs. Even Porkchop stopped eating and began conducting an invisible orchestra with his half-eaten carrot, his face a mask of profound, trance-like ecstasy.

Sir Whiskerton, who had been watching from a safe distance on a dry rock, narrowed his eyes. “This isn’t good,” he murmured. “The fish have hypnotized everyone.”

Ditto, who had been tapping his paw to the rhythm, blinked, shaking his head as if waking from a dream. “Hypnotized? But it’s just music!”

“It’s not just music,” Sir Whiskerton replied, his voice tight. “It’s a spell. And if we don’t break it, the entire farm will be stuck in this aquatic trance forever.”

## III. The Turmoil

With the animals entranced, the farm quickly fell into a state of beautiful, chaotic disarray. 

The chickens stopped laying eggs and started forming a chorus line by the coop. The cows stopped chewing their cud and began singing barbershop quartets. The sheep wandered off into the woods, baaing in perfect, haunting harmony. 

Even the Farmer, who had been watching the show from his rocking chair on the porch, was now yodeling along with the fish, completely oblivious to the fact that his coffee was overflowing onto his boots.

Sir Whiskerton and Ditto tried to snap the animals out of their trance. They shook Rufus. They shouted at Doris. They even tried splashing pond water on Porkchop. But the animals just kept yodeling, waltzing, and conducting, entirely lost in the spotlight of their own minds.

“We need to break the spell,” Sir Whiskerton said, pacing back and forth, his kilt rippling in the evening breeze. “But how?”

Ditto thought for a moment, his ears swiveling. “What if we use something louder than the yodeling? Something that can snap them out of it?”

Sir Whiskerton’s eyes lit up. He looked toward the old toolshed. “A kazoo! Of course! The perfect instrument to disrupt a hypnotic melody.”

## IV. The Resolution

Sir Whiskerton and Ditto raced to the barn, rummaging through a box of old, forgotten toys until Ditto triumphantly held up a dusty, slightly bent plastic kazoo. 

They returned to the pond. The Yodeling Fish were still performing, their voices echoing across the farm, holding the barnyard in their aquatic grip. Mr. Wigglesworth was weeping tears of joy, conducting them with his celery baton.

“Here goes nothing,” Sir Whiskerton said. He placed the kazoo to his lips and blew.

The sound was ear-piercing. It was a sharp, discordant, aggressively un-musical *BZZZZZT-SQUEEEE-BZZZZZT* that cut through the yodeling like a rusty knife through butter. 

The effect was instantaneous. 

The animals froze, their eyes wide with shock. The fish stopped yodeling, their mouths hanging open in surprise. The spell shattered like a dropped teacup.

“What… what happened?” Doris asked, shaking her head and adjusting her wire-rimmed spectacles. “Why am I wearing a feather boa?”

“You were hypnotized,” Sir Whiskerton explained, lowering the kazoo. “The yodeling fish put you all in a trance.”

Rufus blinked, looking down at his paws. “I thought I was at a ballroom dancing competition.”

Porkchop looked at his carrot. “I thought I was conducting the London Symphony Orchestra. I felt very important.”

Mr. Wigglesworth, who had been conducting the fish with his celery, looked deeply disappointed. His top hat slipped over his eyes. “But the show was going so well! The fish were stars! They were destined for greatness!”

Sir Whiskerton sighed, a soft, elegant sound. He walked over to the eccentric pig and gently placed a velvet paw on his shoulder. “Mr. Wigglesworth, fame isn’t worth losing control. The farm was in chaos because of your show. Sometimes, it’s better to keep things simple. The fish are happy in the pond. They don’t need a spotlight.”

Mr. Wigglesworth looked at the fish, who were now happily blowing bubbles and doing the backstroke, entirely content to be ordinary. He nodded reluctantly, a small smile returning to his snout. “I suppose you’re right. But maybe… maybe we could do one more show? Just a small one? Perhaps a mime act?”

Sir Whiskerton rolled his eyes. “Fine. But no more hypnosis. And no more celery batons.”

## The Moral (and the Post-Credit Whisper)

**Moral:**  
Fame and fortune may seem appealing, but they’re not worth sacrificing your peace of mind—or your farm’s productivity. Sometimes, the simplest things in life are the most valuable. It is perfectly okay to be ordinary. In fact, ordinary is underrated. Who needs the spotlight when you have a sunny hay bale, a good nap, and a friend to share the quiet with?

As the farm returned to its usual rhythm, the animals went back to their routines. The chickens laid eggs, the cows gave milk, and the sheep wandered back from the woods, looking slightly confused but otherwise unharmed. The Farmer, who had no memory of yodeling, went back to his rocking chair, and Mr. Wigglesworth began planning a new, less hypnotic show for the yodeling fish.

**Post-Credit Scene:**  
Later that night, Sir Whiskerton and Ditto sat on the barn roof, looking up at the stars. 

“Sir Whiskerton?” Ditto asked softly. “Do you think the fish will ever yodel again?”

Sir Whiskerton took the dusty kazoo and tossed it gently into the tall grass. “Oh, they’ll yodel, little echo. But only when they think no one is listening. And that, my dear apprentice, is the only audience that truly matters.”
