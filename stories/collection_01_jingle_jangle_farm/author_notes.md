## A Note Before We Begin

I did not set out to write a universe.

I set out to help my daughter learn English.

That is where all of this started — with a little girl who resisted English like a cat avoiding rain, and a father who wanted to help her fall in love with a language. The stories came later. The characters came later. The farm came later. And by the time it *was* a farm — with its stories and its characters and its rules — I had already written dozens of little tales without knowing that they were *about* anything at all.

These Author's Notes are the *backstories* — the small, true, sometimes mundane things that *prompted* the stories. They are not explanations. They are *origins*. They are the reason I wrote *this* story on *this* particular day, in *this* particular mood, and not some other story entirely.

I've written them in the first person, because these are my notes. They're not meant to be literature. They're meant to be *the truth behind the fable*. Some of the notes are grand — a *kintsugi* moment, a coffeehouse memory. Some of them are small — a joke at a manager's expense, a cat with saber-tooth fangs, a pickle on a fence post. Both kinds matter. Both kinds are *why* the farm exists.

Read them the way you'd read a letter from an old friend. Not for *craft*. For *connection*.

— Robert

## Story 71 — The Valley Chicks

The Valley Chicks were a literary device before they were characters. They were *California valley girls* — the specific cultural archetype of the late 1990s and early 2000s, transposed into the bodies of three sparkling chicks. The "Oh my gosh" cadence, the obsession with aesthetics, the *"this is, like, aggressively organic"* evaluations of a working farm — all of it came from that specific register.

I knew, when I wrote them, that a Chinese middle-school audience might not have a natural frame of reference for valley girls. The cultural touchstones wouldn't land the same way. I considered changing them — making them *different* kinds of chicks, with a *different* register, more accessible to the audience I was writing for.

I decided to leave them as they were.

Why? Because they were so much *fun* to write. There is a particular pleasure in writing dialogue that is *entirely* unlike anything the farm usually contains — a register that exists in *contrast* to the farm's usual rhythms. The Valley Chicks brought a *tempo* to the barnyard that the barnyard had never had. They made the *silences* longer and the *chatter* sharper. They were, in their own way, *a gift* to the collection.

So I left them in. And the Chinese middle-school kids — the ones I eventually taught — *loved them*. They didn't need to know what a valley girl was. They needed to know what it *felt like* when three sparkly chicks walked into a barn and judged the aesthetic of a working farm. That, apparently, translates.

Not every cultural specific translates *literally*. But the *feeling* behind the cultural specific — the *energy*, the *confidence*, the *exuberance* — that *does* translate. It translates every time.

— Robert

## Story 106 — The Snotty Surprise

I taught ESL for years, and I learned early on that *bathroom humor* was the fastest way to a child's attention. The physical comedy, the *nudge-nudge* element, the sheer uncontainable joy of getting to say *"poop"* in a classroom — it was the most reliable teaching tool I had.

I taught *prepositions* using bathroom vocabulary. "Where is the ___?" "It is *inside* the ___. It is *on top of* the ___. It is *below* the ___. It is *here*." The children loved it. They learned *here*, *there*, *inside*, *top*, *on*, *below* — every preposition I needed them to know — through a vocabulary that made them laugh every single time.

The story came from that. Not *literally* — I did not write a story about ESL class. I wrote a story about a farm where a *giant, glistening snot monument* appeared on the barn door, and where the farm's response to it revealed *everything* about what kind of a community the farm is. The *goo* was the *vehicle*. The *warmth* was the *cargo*.

Children of this age — ten to fourteen — *relish* the gross. They laugh about it. They lean into it. And that is *not* a problem to be corrected. It is a *door* to be walked through. If you want a child to learn that *compassion over contagion* is the right response to a sick friend, you cannot start with the compassion. You have to start with the *snot*. Then, once the child is *laughing*, you can show them — quietly — that the *snot* is coming from a *goose who is lonely*.

The laugh gets them in the door. The *loneliness* is what they *remember*.

— Robert

## Story 133 / 113 — The Great Farm Debate

At the time I wrote this story, I was working under a *perfectionist manager* on a *very difficult project*. Every detail had to be optimized. Every metric had to be met. Every process had to be *ideal* — and no process ever was. I spent months watching a system that *should* have worked *fail*, again and again, because it was designed for a *machine* and not for *people*.

Meanwhile, the news was full of loud conversations about *identity* — about freedom, about definition, about who gets to say who they are. Everyone was certain. Everyone had a *system*. Everyone was *sure* that if you just applied the right framework, the world would finally make sense.

The story was my attempt to *think through* all of that. Not to *resolve* it — I don't think it can be resolved — but to *hold* it. To put a rigid duck with a clipboard on one side, and a red-painted chipmunk with a manifesto on the other, and let them *argue*. To let *both* of them be right. To let *both* of them be wrong. To let the farm discover, together, that neither system is *complete*, and that the only thing that *ever* works is a *dance* between them.

I reached for **Chinese history** as the frame — the Five-Year Plans, the Manifesto, the armband, the productivity drive — because it was the language I had available for *this particular kind of system*. Not to *mock* it. Not to *criticize* China. The Five-Year Plans *work*. They have worked. They built a nation.

But they are *plans*. And *any* plan, pushed to its extreme, forgets that it is being *lived by people* — by ducks, and chipmunks, and pigs, and cats. The farm holds that truth. The farm always has. And the story is my attempt to *show* it.

— Robert

## Story 147 — The Case of the Vanished Virtuoso

When I was four or five years old, my parents took me to a beatnik coffeehouse.

I did not understand what was happening. I was too small. But I *remembered* it — the way you remember things at that age, as *impressions* rather than *events*. Adults in black and grey. Berets. Cigarettes. Coffee. Notebooks and pens on every table. Someone standing up and reciting a poem that no one seemed to be listening to, and everyone seemed to *feel*. A low, smoky light. And me, on a counter at the end of the room, watching.

We left. I don't remember why. I don't remember how long we stayed. I only remember the *impression*.

That impression is why *The Case of the Vanished Virtuoso* exists. The subterranean jazz den beneath the carrot patch — the moles in zoot suits, the acorn-cap records, the small, hidden stage — that is not a fantasy. That is a *child's memory* of a room where grown-ups did something important that I was not yet old enough to understand.

I wrote it decades later, still not fully understanding. But I understood *enough* — enough to know that some places are *sacred*, and some rituals *matter*, and some sounds are *older than the people making them*. The farm has a jazz den because I was four years old once, and I saw something I could not yet name.

Not every story needs a moral. Not every story needs a post-credit scene. Some stories are just *the thing that happened*, remembered from a counter, decades later, by someone who finally found a way to describe the feeling.

— Robert

## Story 188 — The Arrival of Count Catula

I lost a cat once.

His name was **Smokey**. He was not black. He was *charcoal* — a lush, deep, short charcoal grey that seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it. I called him my *little toke*, which was a small joke, an affection, a name that meant *him and only him*. He had the most magnificent fangs — long, elegant, protruding past his lower lip in a way that made him look like he had walked straight out of a Victorian vampire novel. He did not *look* like a housecat. He looked like a *character*. And he *knew it*.

Smokey communicated in dramatic flair. He did not *ask* for things. He *announced* them. He did not *want* attention. He *required* it. He would enter a room like a count arriving at a ball, pause at the threshold, survey the company, and only *then* descend. If you were lucky, he would deign to sit on your lap. If you were *unlucky*, he would sit *near* you and stare at you with an expression that suggested he was *tolerating* your existence. He was, in every sense of the word, a *drama queen*.

I wrote Count Catula as a tribute to him. The black cape, the fangs, the carefully rehearsed theatrical voice, the *"I shall require... a probationary haunting"* — all of it was Smokey. All of it was *him*.

But then something happened that I still regret. I asked an AI to *clean up* the story. I meant *grammar*. I meant *punctuation*. I meant *rhythm*. Instead, the AI *flattened* him. It took Smokey — complex, dramatic, sincere, fragile — and turned him into a *two-dimensional comic cardboard cutout*. A "vampire cat" with a cape and a punchline. All of the *layers* were gone. All of the *tenderness beneath the theatrics* was gone. The character was still technically *there*, but he was *not Smokey anymore*.

I lost the original story. I could not recover it. So I had to *rewrite it from memory* — and the rewrite forced me to *understand* Smokey better than I had before. I wrote a *bio* of Count Catula. I traced his *psychology*. I found the wound underneath the dramatics — the fear of being *unwelcome*, the performance that was *not an act* but a *shield*. And when I rewrote the story, I wrote *that* Smokey. Not the joke version. The *real* one.

Count Catula is not a comic. He never was. He is a cat who *communicates in dramatic flair* because dramatic flair is the *only register* he is comfortable in. He is a cat who *asks for a probationary haunting* because he is *afraid to ask for a home*. He is a cat who *holds his cape between himself and Ditto* — not to drape it, not to wear it, but to hold it *between them* — because that is *the closest thing to trust* he knows how to offer.

Smokey would have approved. Or, more likely, he would have *pretended* not to notice — and then, later, when he thought no one was looking, he would have nudged the cape with his head.

— Robert

## Story 273 — The Case of the Phantom Pickle

Pickles startle cats. I don't know why. Nobody knows why. But every cat I have ever owned has *reacted* to a pickle as if it were a *threat*. The tail puffs. The ears flatten. The cat *jumps sideways*, *hisses*, *runs*. It is not *logical*. It is not *learned*. It is *instinct*, and the instinct is *completely inexplicable*, and it is *both poignant and funny at the same time*.

The story came from watching this happen. Again. And again. And again. My cats were afraid of pickles, and I could not *explain it*, and I could not *fix it*, and I could not *stop laughing about it*.

But I did not want to write a story that *mocked* the fear. I wanted to write a story that *honored* it. Because the fear is *real*. It is *not a bit*. The cat is *genuinely*, *sincerely* terrified. And the *honor* the farm shows Whiskerton for that fear — the way no one *ever* mocks him for it, the way his *dignity* is *preserved* even as his *absurdity* is *acknowledged* — that is what the story is *about*.

The pickle is not the villain. The pickle is *never* the villain. The pickle is *just a cucumber slice*, and the cat is *just a cat*, and the *fear* is *just a fear*, and *nobody on the farm makes it into anything more than that*. That is the story. That is *why* the story works.

— Robert

## A Final Note

Most of these stories were written during a period of personal chaos. I was a CEO. I was a GM. I was a father. I was dealing with pressures that I could not always name, on timelines that were not my own, in systems that did not always work.

The farm was my escape. It was the place I went when the world outside was too loud. It was the place I *built* — one story at a time, one character at a time, one small moral at a time — because I *needed* a place to go.

That is why the farm is *soft*. That is why the farm is *safe*. That is why nothing truly dies here, and why nobody is ever exiled, and why every conflict resolves into a deeper understanding. Because I was *making* the world I *needed* to live in, and the world I needed was one where *belonging was not conditional*.

I made many mistakes along the way. There are typos in the early drafts that make me wince. There are grammatical errors I will never fully fix. There are sentences that run too long, and moments that run too short, and characters that took three drafts to *become themselves*. The mess is *part* of the work. The mess is *evidence* of the work — of a real person, in real life, doing something that mattered to them.

Some of the notes above are *grand* — a coffeehouse memory, a cat with saber-tooth fangs, a manager I could not please. Some of them are *mundane* — a joke about prepositions, a pickle on a fence post, a valley girl accent from the 1990s. Both are true. Both are *mine*. Both are *why* the farm exists.

The farm is not a franchise. It is not a brand. It is a *place*, and I built it in the cracks of a busy life, because I needed somewhere to *be*.

If you are reading these notes, you have probably already read the stories. Thank you for caring about the *why* behind the *what*. Thank you for stepping into this small, true, silliest corner of my life.

I hope it feels like home to you.

— Robert
