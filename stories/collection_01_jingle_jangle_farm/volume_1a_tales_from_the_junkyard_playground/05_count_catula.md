# Sir Whiskerton and the Arrival of Count Catula

*A Tale of Capes, Courage, and the Quiet Kind of Magic*

*From the Collection 1 archive — Story 188*

---

Ah, dear reader—tonight's story does not begin with fog, nor thunder, nor even a bat-shaped hot-air balloon (though one was rented, and later returned, slightly singed). It begins with a shadow that moved too slowly to be danger. With a cape that billowed not in the wind—but in the breath of someone trying very hard to be seen... and even harder not to be known.

## Chapter 1: The Shadow at the Gate

The evening was soft—a lavender dusk, the kind that invites reflection. Sir Whiskerton sat on his porch roof, watching fireflies sketch constellations in the clover. Below, Ditto practiced balancing on the fence, whispering to himself: "Brave... brave... brave!"

Then—a ripple in the calm.

At the farm's western gate—a rusted iron arch strung with honeysuckle—stood a figure.

Tall. Still.

Cloaked in black velvet, lined with crimson like a sunset caught in fabric. The wind stirred, and the cape lifted, not dramatically, but hesitantly—as if unsure it was welcome.

The figure stepped forward.

A cat. All black, sleek as spilled ink, save for two pale fangs—longer than most, yes, but not monstrous. Just... noticeable. And his eyes—wide, luminous green—not glowing, exactly, but catching the last light like polished sea glass. He carried a small valise. And a silence heavier than luggage.

He did not enter.

He waited.

Ditto saw him first.

"Shadow!" he chirped—then, correcting himself before the word could settle: "Cat!"

The stranger flinched—just slightly—as if the word had startled him. Then, with a flourish so practiced it trembled at the edges, he swept the cape over one shoulder, bared his fangs (a reflex, not a threat), and cleared his throat.

His voice, when it came, was low, rich, and carefully theatrical—like a radio actor reading poetry at 2 a.m.:

"I am... Count Catula."

A pause. A breath.

"Lord of the Longing Night. Keeper of the Unspoken Hours. And... occasional admirer of fine dairy."

He waited.

No one laughed.

No one fled.

Sir Whiskerton simply descended, landing silently on the path before him.

"You've come a long way," Whiskerton observed. Not a question. An offering.

Catula's ear twitched. "The road was... poorly lit. And populated by overly inquisitive crows. One demanded a résumé."

"Crows are traditionalists," Whiskerton said, offering a small bow. "But we are not. You're welcome here—if you wish to be."

Catula's eyes flickered—not with suspicion, but something softer. Relief? Dread? He gripped the valise tighter.

"I do not wish," he said, voice dropping, the performance thinning for half a second. "I require... a trial. A... probationary haunting."

Whiskerton tilted his head. "Of the barn?"

"Of the atmosphere," Catula corrected, regaining his cadence—though his tail betrayed him, curling tight around his leg, like a question mark made of fur. "I shall occupy the owl box in the old oak. I shall emerge only at dusk and dawn. I shall speak only in iambic couplets—or, if pressed, haiku. And should any creature dare approach me uninvited..."

He paused. Swallowed.

"...they shall be met with theatrics of such magnitude, they will beg for daylight."

Whiskerton nodded, as if this were perfectly reasonable.

"Accepted. With one amendment."

Catula stiffened. "Yes?"

"You may keep the cape."

A beat.

"And you needn't haunt alone."

Catula's breath hitched—just once.

Then he bowed, so deeply his nose brushed the dirt.

"The night thanks you," he murmured.

And vanished—not in smoke, but in quiet—up the gnarled trunk of the oak, the valise bumping softly against the bark.

Ditto stared.

"Alone?" he echoed—softly, thoughtfully.

## Chapter 2: The First Overture

For three nights, Catula did not descend.

The farm whispered.

*Is he real? Did he melt? Did he turn into mist and float back to wherever vampires come from?* (Which, Doris theorized, was "Definitely Wisconsin.")

But Whiskerton knew: he was there. Every dusk, a faint hum drifted from the owl box—not singing. Humming. A lullaby. Off-key. Tender.

Then—on the fourth evening—as the sky bled into indigo, Ditto did what no one else dared.

He climbed the oak.

Not stealthily. Not dramatically. Just... up. Paws scrabbling, tail wobbling, heart loud in his ears.

He found Catula on the box's sill, cape pooled around him like a dark pond, staring at the horizon. His fangs were hidden. His eyes were tired.

Ditto sat beside him. Not too close. Just... near.

A long silence. The wind rustled the leaves.

Then Ditto tilted his head, looked at the cape—its frayed hem, its soft inner lining, the faint scent of old books and bergamot—and asked, in the clearest, most unafraid voice in the world:

## "Can I wear the cape?"

Catula froze.

Not in alarm.

In wonder.

He turned—slowly—and for the first time, his gaze didn't perform. It rested. On Ditto. On his wide, silver eyes. On the trust held there, simple as a sunbeam.

He didn't speak. Didn't strike a pose. Didn't even smile.

But he reached out—one black paw—and with infinite care, lifted the edge of the cape.

Not to drape it on Ditto.

But to hold it between them.

Like a bridge.

Like a promise.

And in that suspended moment—no fangs, no fanfare, no "Count"—just two cats and a length of velvet in the gathering dark—

## the haunting ended.

The belonging began.

## Epilogue: The Lord of Nighttime Naps

Count Catula did not stop being theatrical.

Why would he? The world is brighter for a little drama.

But the cape, now, has a second wearer.

Ditto wears it for story time.

Bessie borrowed it once for a "moody photoshoot" (it clashed with her mood ring—glorious).

Even Jazzpurr tried it on—paired with his turtleneck and a beret—and called it "Gothic Beatnik Chic."

Catula still hosts The Dusk Club—but now, the first rule is:

"No soliloquies until everyone has had tea."

And when the fireflies rise, and the farm settles into its soft, shared hush, you might find him on the porch roof beside Sir Whiskerton—cape folded neatly beside him, fangs hidden, eyes reflecting the stars—not as a lord of night, but as a friend who finally learned:

You don't need to be feared to be safe. You only need to be seen—and met, gently, where you stand.

The End.

## Post-Credit Scene

A month after Catula's arrival, a small invitation appears on the farm's community board — a single card, written in careful, looping script:

> *The Dusk Club*
> *Meets at Sunset*
> *The Old Oak*
> *Dress: Optional*
> *Cape: Provided*
> *Theatrics: Encouraged*
> *Tea: Mandatory*

The first gathering draws only three guests: Ditto, in the cape; Jazzpurr, in his beret; and — unexpectedly — Bessie the Tie-Dye Cow, who arrives with her mood ring glowing a deep, satisfied violet.

They sit in a loose circle at the base of the oak. Catula reads a poem. Ditto applauds too loudly. Jazzpurr snaps his claws in beatnik approval. Bessie hums an old song about clover.

The farm, from a distance, looks exactly as it always has. Nothing has changed.

Everything has changed.

And high on the porch roof, Sir Whiskerton watches the small, quiet circle of friends gather in the dark — and does not, as he so often does, comment. He simply closes his eyes, settles into his sunbeam's memory, and purrs.

## Key Jokes

- **"Doris theorized, was 'Definitely Wisconsin.'"** — the farm's frantic speculation about *where vampires come from* ends with Doris confidently deciding *Wisconsin*. The joke is that in a crisis, the farm's most dramatic hen defaults to *regional American geography* as her category for the supernatural.
- **"One demanded a résumé."** — Catula's *casual aside* about his journey, describing a crow who interrogated him for paperwork. The joke is that even *vampires* have to deal with bureaucracy, and Catula is too theatrical to complain about it plainly.
- **"And you needn't haunt alone."** — Sir Whiskerton's amendment to Catula's "probationary haunting" terms. The joke is the *inversion*: Catula expected to *haunt* the farm; instead, the farm *invites him in*. The word "haunt" is retained, but given an entirely different meaning.
- **"Can I wear the cape?"** — the single, unafraid question that *breaks* Catula's performance. The joke is that after *three nights* of the farm whispering about whether he was real, the moment of connection comes from a tiny kitten who skips past all the mystique and *asks for the cape*. It's the *most direct possible* response to Catula's theatricality.
- **"No soliloquies until everyone has had tea."** — the first rule of the Dusk Club, and the perfect *domestication* of Catula's drama. The joke is that even *vampire poetry nights* on the farm now run on *tea and etiquette*.
- **"Gothic Beatnik Chic"** — Jazzpurr's *aesthetic category* for the cape, which he wears paired with a turtleneck and beret. The joke is that Jazzpurr, the farm's most committed *artiste*, interprets Catula's vampiric signature as *just another fashion statement*.
- **"The night thanks you."** — Catula's parting line when he accepts the invitation, delivered with *total solemnity*. The joke is that he has just been *welcomed into a family*, and he insists on *bowing deeply* and *naming himself a representative of the night*. He is, in every way, still performing — and no one asks him to stop. That is the farm's love.

## Starring

- **Count Catula** (吸血鬼猫) — the arriving vampire cat, theatrical, anxious, and longing not to be feared but to be *seen*.
- **Sir Whiskerton** (胡子爵士) — the host at the gate, whose simple offering — "you're welcome here, if you wish to be" — is the story's entire moral, enacted.
- **Ditto the Kitten** (复读猫) — the one who climbs the oak, refuses to perform, and asks the question that *breaks the spell*: "Can I wear the cape?"
- **Doris the Hen** (八卦母鸡) — the farm's most reliable speculator, whose guess at the vampire's origin is *Wisconsin*.
- **Bessie the Cow** (彩虹牛) — the surprise guest at the first Dusk Club, whose mood ring glows *deep satisfied violet*.
- **Jazzpurr** (爵士猫) — the beatnik aesthetician who wears the cape "Gothic Beatnik Chic" and snaps his claws in approval.
- **The Farmer** (农场主) — absent from this story, but present in spirit: the farm is what it is *because* of him.

## P.S.

The farm has space for anyone who is willing to be *seen* — even the ones who arrive dressed as something else entirely.

Count Catula came as a vampire. He left as a friend. He came to haunt a barn. He left with a *cape* and a *tea club* and a *kitten* who wears his velvet like a crown.

Nothing about him changed. He is still theatrical. He is still "the night." But the farm *held him* until he stopped needing to announce himself — and then, quietly, let him be.

That is what belonging looks like. Not a transformation. A *permission*.

He wears the cape. He plays the part. And now, when the fireflies rise, he sits beside Sir Whiskerton on the porch roof — fangs hidden, eyes on the stars — and does not, for the first time in a long time, feel the need to perform.

The night thanks him. The farm thanks him. And he, finally, thanks *them*.
