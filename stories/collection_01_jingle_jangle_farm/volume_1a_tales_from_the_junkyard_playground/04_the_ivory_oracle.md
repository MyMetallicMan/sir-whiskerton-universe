# Sir Whiskerton and the Ivory Oracle

*A Tale of Tiles, Trajectories, and Trust*

*From the Collection 1 archive — Story 110*

---

Ah, dear reader. Sit. Be still, if you can. The story I bring you today hums with a different frequency—not the clang of chaos or the buzz of mischief, but the soft, persistent click of ivory on oak, the sound of fate being arranged into tidy rows. It is the story of how a farm, governed by the heart and the mind, learned to make room for the soul of a prophet with quills. Prepare for a quiet revelation, as we explore Sir Whiskerton and the Ivory Oracle.

## I. The Arrival of the Living Algorithm

It was a day of perfect equilibrium. The Farmer was engaged in a deeply philosophical conversation with a wheelbarrow about its "unidirectional ambitions." Beatrice's bees wove contentment into the air. Sir Whiskerton, from his roof, observed a universe in harmonious, predictable motion.

The disruption was not a sound, but a silence that absorbed sound.

Beneath the great oak, where dappled light fell like scattered coins, a new figure had manifested. He was a specter in reverse—not a shadow, but a concentration of light, an albino porcupine whose quills glowed like bone china. Upon his head sat a bowler hat, brushed to a muted sheen, an island of solemnity atop a sea of ivory spines. Before him, arrayed with geometric perfection on a cloth of faded velvet, were 144 mahjong tiles.

His pale pink eyes were closed. His paws moved over the tiles not with haste, but with the sacerdotal grace of a priest at an altar, turning, touching, listening. The click-clack-click was a language unto itself.

The animals gathered, not with raucous curiosity, but with a hushed, reverential confusion. This was no bumbling visitor. This was an *event*.

"I am Percy," he said, his voice a dry rustle, like pages turning in an old ledger. He did not open his eyes. "And this is the board of what is, what was, and what is becoming."

Doris the Hen puffed up, affronted by such certainty. "A board game? We have duties."

"It is not a game," Percy replied, finally fixing her with his unsettling gaze. "It is a lens. For instance, Doris. The One of Bamboo lies adjacent to the White Dragon. A straight line interrupted by purity. You will, within the hour, discover your prized russet egg has been laid... perfectly spotless. You will find this aesthetically displeasing."

Doris squawked in derision. Yet, an hour later, her cry of frustrated discovery echoed across the yard. The egg was immaculate, shell without blemish. To Doris, it was an outrage. To the others, it was a miracle of minutiae.

## II. The Farm Recalibrated

Thus began the reign of the Ivory Oracle. Percy's pronouncements, delivered in his calm, rustling voice, became the farm's new nervous system.

He did not predict. He *calculated trajectories*.

To Porkchop: "The Nine of Circles is warm from the sun. The mash tonight will be 4.7 degrees hotter than optimal. Approach with pause."

To Bessie the Cow: "The East Wind tile has a hairline crack. A change is coming from the eastern pasture. Your mood ring will shift to a contemplative turquoise before noon."

To The Farmer, who had paused to listen: "Your left boot's lace fibre is undergoing incremental fatigue. Failure is 97% probable within 36 hours. The Red Dragon suggests the site will be the pumpkin patch."

The Farmer nodded slowly, as if receiving the weather report. "Pumpkins are good listeners," he murmured, and ambled away, forgetting to check his laces.

Sir Whiskerton observed it all, his detective's mind a whirlwind of analysis. Coincidence? Pattern recognition? Or something deeper? Percy operated on a logic so refined it appeared to be magic. He claimed no supernatural power. Only attention, woven across 8,000 hours of silent observation.

"Luck," Percy would often murmur to his tiles, "is merely preparation meeting opportunity." For him, the entire farm was a sprawling game of mahjong, every creature a tile with predictable properties, every action a discard that set up the next play.

## III. The Crack in the Ivory

The crisis arose from a prediction that touched the farm's heart.

"The hive at the lavender border," Percy stated, his paw resting on the Green Dragon tile, a symbol of growth and health, "shows a discordant pattern. The bees' flight paths are the One of Circles, but their return is the Three of Bamboo—asynchronous. There is a sickness within. It will manifest in honey that tastes of... distance."

Beatrice, who had welcomed Percy's insights on weather and bloom cycles, went pale. Her bees? Her beloved daughters? She rushed to the hive, her gentle hands conducting a careful inspection. She found no overt sickness, no foulbrood, no failing queen. Yet, a subtle unease persisted. Percy's words had planted a seed of diagnostic doubt.

Worse, he made a prediction concerning Sir Whiskerton himself. "The monocle will be clouded at the precise moment truth requires clarity. A flower tile obscured by a season tile."

This was no longer charming. It was an assault on agency. If the future was simply a tile waiting to be revealed, what was the point of deduction, of care, of choice? Percy's beautiful, terrifying logic threatened to reduce their rich, emotional lives to predetermined moves.

Sir Whiskerton confronted him under the oak. The tiles were between them, a wall of carved possibilities.

"You traffic in certainty, Percy," Sir Whiskerton said, his voice low. "You see patterns and call them fate. You diagnose a hive you have never touched, and cloud a heart with fear. Your quills are not just on your back."

Percy's pink eyes met the cat's steady gaze. "I read the board, Sir Whiskerton. Nothing more. The hive is out of rhythm. Your monocle will fail. I do not cause these things. I merely... notice the arithmetic of their approach."

"But you announce it!" Sir Whiskerton's tail gave a single, sharp flick. "You speak the sum aloud before the equation is finished being lived. You rob the present of its mystery, and the future of its fight."

## IV. The Showdown: Logic vs. The Living Tapestry

A challenge was inevitable. Not of strength, but of philosophy.

"A game," Sir Whiskerton proposed. "Your mahjong. We play. But we do not play for tiles. We play for the narrative of the farm."

The terms were set. Percy would use his 8,000-hour memory, his flawless probabilistic logic. Sir Whiskerton would play not to win the game, but to *disrupt the prediction*. His secret weapon was not a tile, but a variable Percy could never fully compute: **Ditto**.

The game began at twilight. Percy was a maestro, his moves swift, silent, inevitable. He built walls of certainty. Sir Whiskerton played a defensive, observant game, but his true strategy was psychological.

"Ditto," he purred mid-game, "the White Dragon tile looks remarkably like a miniature square of cheese, does it not?"

"CHEESE!" Ditto yelped, immediately fixating on the tile.

Percy's concentration, a cathedral of silent logic, developed its first hairline crack—not from error, but from wonder.

He didn't touch it, but his vibrating, eager presence by the board was a hurricane of unpredictable energy.

Percy's concentration, which relied on a field of perfect calm, developed its first hairline crack.

Sir Whiskerton then began to narrate. "I discard the Four of Circles," he said. "Which, if I recall your system, Percy, relates to the cyclical nature of... Rufus's nap schedule. He is due to twitch in his sleep... now."

On cue, Rufus let out a muffled 'woof!' from his dreams. Percy's whiskers twitched.

The cat wove the living, breathing, messy farm into the game. He connected tiles to Beatrice's humming, to the scent of rain on The Farmer's shirt, to the distant, lonely honk of Wilma the Goose. He injected the humanity—the beautiful, irrational, caring chaos—that Percy's pristine logic excluded.

Percy, flustered, made a rare miscalculation. He called for a tile that was already in the discard pile.

The game did not end with a dramatic win. It ended with Percy setting down his final tile not in triumph, but in quiet astonishment. He had been defeated not by a superior strategist, but by a superior reality—one that included love, distraction, empathy, and a dog dreaming of rabbits.

## V. The Moral, Carved in Ivory and Heart

Percy looked at the board, then at the farm around him—at Beatrice now smiling as her bees returned in a harmonious cloud, at The Farmer successfully tying both boots, at the imperfect, glorious tapestry of it all.

"I... misread the board," he said, his rustling voice softer than ever. "The most powerful tile was not on the table. It is the Wild Tile. The unpredictable variable of... care. It changes all the equations."

He did not stop observing. He did not stop calculating. But he learned to temper his prophecies with poetry.

He no longer said, "The hive is sick." He began to say, "The lavender hive sings a slightly lonely song. Perhaps a melody of extra thyme blossoms nearby would harmonize them."

He no longer predicted The Farmer's trips. He would simply, quietly, tie the loose lace himself as The Farmer philosophized with a turnip.

The Moral of the Story, dear reader, is this: We live our lives on the board between destiny and chance. Some souls are gifted at reading the patterns laid down by yesterday's choices. But the most important moves—the ones of kindness, intervention, and shared laughter—are the wild tiles we choose to play today. True wisdom lies not in seeing the inevitable, but in using that sight to gently, lovingly alter the game.

## Epilogue: The New Harmony

Percy remains beneath the oak, his tiles clicking their soft song. He is no longer the feared Oracle, but the Respected Analyst. He and Sir Whiskerton have reached a detente: one maps the currents, the other sails the ship. Sometimes, they play a quiet game. Sir Whiskerton never wins on tile-points alone. But he always wins the game of reminding Percy that a paw offered, a warning softened, or a joke shared with Ditto is the most potent strategy of all.

For Percy learned that while luck is preparation meeting opportunity, grace is observation meeting compassion. And on this farm, grace is the only prediction that ever truly comes to pass.

The End.

## Post-Credit Scene

Late at night, long after the farm has settled, Percy sits alone beneath the oak. He is not playing mahjong. His tiles are packed neatly away in their velvet cloth.

He is watching the stars.

Above him, the familiar constellations turn on their slow axis. He could calculate their arcs. He could predict — with fair precision — when the moon will rise, when the first frost will fall, when the next summer will arrive.

But tonight, he simply watches. And for the first time since arriving on the farm, he finds that he does not need to know what comes next.

Somewhere behind him, soft footsteps pad across the grass. Sir Whiskerton settles beside him without a word. The two of them sit, side by side, in the quiet. Neither speaks.

After a long while, Percy says, softly: *"The stars are not tiles."*

Sir Whiskerton does not answer.

He does not need to.

The stars are not tiles. And that is exactly the point.

## Key Jokes

- **Percy's bedside manner** — his first prediction to Doris is delivered not as prophecy but as a *diagnostic bulletin*: *"You will, within the hour, discover your prized russet egg has been laid... perfectly spotless. You will find this aesthetically displeasing."* The joke is that he *knows what she will care about* before she knows it herself.
- **"The mash tonight will be 4.7 degrees hotter than optimal"** — a perfect, tiny, absurd prediction aimed at Porkchop the Pig. The joke is that in a universe of cosmic mysteries, the oracle's most reliable insight is about *the temperature of a pig's dinner*.
- **The Farmer's response to prophecy** — *"Pumpkins are good listeners."* The joke is that the Farmer is *completely unfazed* by Percy's prediction that his shoelace will break in the pumpkin patch. He treats prophecy as weather, and weather as a topic of philosophical conversation.
- **The "8,000-hour memory"** — a mock-technical term for Percy's accumulated observation. The joke is that *mystical foresight* is rebranded as *rigorous record-keeping*, and it works — because it is.
- **Ditto as the wild tile** — Sir Whiskerton uses Ditto's *cheese fixation* to break Percy's concentration. The joke is that the undoing of an 8,000-hour predictive model is a kitten shouting "CHEESE!" at a mahjong tile.
- **Percy's softened prophecies** — after his defeat, he learns to speak in poetry instead of prediction: *"The lavender hive sings a slightly lonely song."* The joke is that the most accurate oracle on the farm becomes *more accurate* when he stops trying to be right.
- **"The stars are not tiles"** — the closing line of the post-credit scene, a perfect summation of Percy's arc. The joke — and it is a gentle one — is that after a lifetime of *reading* the universe, he finally understands that some things are not meant to be *read*.

## Starring

- **Percy the Albino Porcupine** (白豪猪珀西) — the Ivory Oracle. The farm's most rigorous observer, who must learn that logic without compassion is just prediction, and prediction without grace is just noise.
- **Sir Whiskerton** (胡子爵士) — the philosopher-detective who defeats prophecy not with a better prediction, but by reminding it that *love is unpredictable*.
- **Ditto the Kitten** (复读猫) — the wild tile. The variable that cannot be computed. His obsession with "cheese" is what breaks the oracle.
- **Doris the Hen** (八卦母鸡) — the first subject of Percy's predictions, whose outrage at a "perfectly spotless egg" is *exactly* what the oracle foretold.
- **Bessie the Cow** (彩虹牛) — the recipient of a "contemplative turquoise" mood-ring prediction, which unfolds exactly on schedule.
- **Porkchop the Pig** (猪大排) — the recipient of the mash-temperature prediction, whose dinner is *4.7 degrees hotter than optimal*.
- **Beatrice** (笨笨蜜姐) — whose beloved bees are the subject of Percy's *one prediction that might hurt*, and whose trust is the reason his *eventual poetry* is so valuable.
- **The Farmer** (农场主) — the calm recipient of prophecy, who treats the oracle as weather and weather as philosophy.

## P.S.

The farm does not need a prophet. It needs *someone paying attention*. Percy's gift was never magic. It was the willingness to notice — to *actually* look at the world, hour after hour, and *remember* what he saw.

What he had to learn — what all oracles must learn — is that *attention without tenderness becomes a kind of cruelty*. A prediction of doom is a curse, even if it is true. A prediction of hope is a *gift*, even if it is uncertain.

Percy chose the gift. He chose the poetry. And on a farm where nothing is ever quite predetermined, that is the safest prediction of all.
