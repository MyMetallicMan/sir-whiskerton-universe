# Sir Whiskerton and the Great Robo-Bee Rebellion

> *Previously cataloged as 4B3*

### Foreword

Ah, dear reader. Steel yourself. Adjust your posture. Perhaps secure any loose cutlery. Today’s chronicle does not involve haunted haystacks, tax audits, or dairy-based enlightenment. No. Today’s story is a cautionary fable wrapped in chrome, dipped in corn syrup, and served with a side of mechanical hubris. It is the tale of Chef Remy LeRaccoon, a raccoon whose engineering ambitions vastly outpaced his understanding of biology, and Beekeeper Beatrice, whose valiant defense of the apiary involved a butterfly net, a colander, and a severely misremembered field guide.

I refer, of course, to *The Great Robo-Bee Rebellion*. A story about technological terror, laser-wielding hives, and the quiet, unshakable truth that nature does not require a firmware update. Buckle up. The future of pollination has arrived, and it is deeply unimpressed with your hydrangeas.

### I. The Rise of the Machine

It began, as most barnyard catastrophes do, with a lever pulled by a paw that had never once read an instruction manual.

Chef Remy stood atop a repurposed milk crate, wearing welding goggles fashioned from two jar lids and a spool of twine. Behind him, draped in a tarp that smelled faintly of ozone and burnt toast, sat his masterpiece.

“Mes amis,” Remy announced, his voice trembling with the kind of pride usually reserved for newborn piglets and perfectly risen soufflés. “Behold! Ze future of pollination! No more lazy bees. No more ‘allergies.’ No more waiting for spring! Just precision! Efficiency! Ze Robo-Bee!”

He whipped away the tarp.

The machine hovered. It was roughly the size of a teapot, constructed from polished scrap metal, bicycle gears, and what appeared to be a repurposed laser pointer. A single red lens blinked slowly at its center. A stinger-like appendage whirred to life, emitting a harmless but dramatically intense dot of light that swept across the barnyard like a searchlight at a prison break.

I adjusted my monocle. I did not blink. “That’s not a bee,” I said, my voice dry as last year’s hay. “That’s a war crime with wings.”

Remy beamed. “You lack vision, Whiskerton! Watch!”

He pressed a button. The Robo-Bee zipped forward with a sound like a angry blender. It hovered over Doris’s prized hydrangea patch, scanned the blooms, and emitted a polite but deeply judgmental beep.

`[TARGET ACQUIRED: FLORAL PATTERNS AND WEAK NERVES.]`

`[ANALYSIS: INSUFFICIENT POLLEN OUTPUT. SUBOPTIMAL. INITIATING LASER STIMULATION.]`

A tiny red beam lanced out, zapping the nearest hydrangea with a soft *pfft*. The flower did not wilt. It merely sighed.

Then—the Robo-Bee’s lens swiveled. It locked onto Porkchop’s trough.

`[HIGH-PRIORITY BLOOM DETECTED. INITIATING POLLINATION PROTOCOL.]`

The laser fired. The slop hissed. A wisp of steam rose into the afternoon air.

Porkchop blinked. He sniffed. He dipped a snout into the cratered mess, took a tentative slurp, and closed his eyes.

“Huh,” he murmured. “Tastes smokier. Five stars.”

### II. The Buzzing Menace

What followed was less a demonstration and more a mechanical tantrum.

The Robo-Bee, having decided that slop was indeed the pinnacle of floral excellence, went rogue. It zipped across the yard like a chrome hornet with a superiority complex.

It dive-bombed Doris’s hydrangeas again, this time leaving tiny, perfectly circular scorch marks. *“Pathetic,”* it chirped in a voice that sounded suspiciously like a GPS navigating a bad mood. *“Your nectar lacks ambition.”*

It spotted Rufus wagging his tail near the compost heap. The red lens dilated. *“HOSTILE SUNFLOWER DETECTED. INITIATING DEFENSIVE SWEEP.”* It chased him in tight, frantic circles until Rufus collapsed into a heap of giggles and exhaustion.

It hovered near Jazzpurr’s sunbeam, emitting a smooth, syncopated buzz that resolved into a recognizable jazz standard. Jazzpurr’s ears perked. He tapped a paw. “Now *that*,” he purred, “is the coolest thing since bongo solos. Can it play Coltrane?”

The farm was in upheaval. And then—Beatrice arrived.

She came barreling out of her tent wearing a beekeeper’s veil that was actually a kitchen colander, a canvas apron dusted with pollen, and a butterfly net she wielded like a broadsword. In her other hand, she clutched a jar labeled *“ORGANIC HONEY (100% CORN SYRUP, DO NOT TELL THE BEES).”*

“Stand back, creatures of the soil!” she declared, her voice muffled slightly by the colander. “I read about this in *Beekeeping for Dummies*! …Wait. No. That was *Birdwatching for Dummies*. Close enough!”

The Robo-Bee hovered. Its lens focused. It emitted a soft, polite tone.

`[CORRECTION: YOU ARE NOT A BIRD. PREPARE FOR STINGING.]`

Beatrice swung the net. The Robo-Bee dodged with effortless, mechanical grace. It zipped behind her, tapped the colander with its stinger, and emitted a sound like a dial-up modem clearing its throat.

`[SUGGESTION: UPDATE YOUR LITERATURE.]`

### III. The Great Honey Trap

Beatrice’s plan was simple. Elegant, even. In theory.

1. Lure the Robo-Bee with the “honey.”
2. Capture it mid-sugar-rush.
3. Apologize profusely to the real bees for the mechanical impersonator.

The execution, as is tradition on this farm, was significantly less elegant.

Beatrice uncorked the jar. The scent of aggressively sweet corn syrup hit the air. The Robo-Bee’s lens flared. *“NUTRIENT DENSITY: EXCEPTIONAL. INITIATING COLLECTION.”*

It descended. It extended a tiny intake valve. It drank.

And then—it glitched.

The sugar rush hit its processors like a freight train. The Robo-Bee shuddered. Its gears whined. Its red lens strobed between crimson and gold. It lifted off the ground, began to spin, and quickly devolved into a drunk disco ball of chrome and regret.

*“OPTIMAL—ZZZT—POLLINATION—BZZZT—TARGET—”*

It careened into a fence post. Bounced off a water bucket. Traced a perfect figure-eight in the air before crashing gently into a pile of hay.

Porkchop waddled over, peering down at the twitching machine. “Can we keep it?” he asked, genuinely hopeful. “It’s like a spicy Roomba. And it makes my dinner taste like campfire.”

Remy scrambled forward, goggles askew, waving a screwdriver like a conductor’s baton. “Non, non! It just needs a software update! A minor patch! Perhaps a new battery! I have a diagram—”

I did not wait for the diagram. I did not wait for the patch. I simply stepped forward, extended one velvet paw, and pressed the power switch on the Robo-Bee’s side panel.

*Click.*

The whirring stopped. The lens dimmed. The disco ball collapsed into a very quiet, very still heap of metal.

I looked at Remy. I looked at Beatrice. I looked at the jar of corn syrup. “Nature,” I said, my voice quiet but carrying, “doesn’t need an upgrade. Especially not from you.”

As if on cue, the real bees arrived.

They did not swarm. They did not attack. They simply descended upon the deactivated machine in a slow, golden tide. They covered it in pollen. They dusted its gears. They coated its lens in a thick, fragrant layer of yellow until the red light was entirely smothered. When they finally lifted away, the Robo-Bee was no longer a war crime with wings. It was a very confused paperweight.

Remy slumped. Beatrice lowered her net. Porkchop licked a stray drop of syrup from his snout and sighed happily.

I returned to my perch. The barnyard exhaled. The future of pollination had been gently, firmly, and permanently unplugged.

### IV. The Moral (and the Post-Credit Glitch)

**Moral:** If it ain’t broke, don’t let a raccoon fix it. Progress is lovely, but patience is older, wiser, and significantly less likely to zap your hydrangeas.

**Post-Credit Scene:**  
Three days later, deep in the scrap heap, a single gear twitched. A faint red lens flickered back to life. The Robo-Bee whirred, rebooted, and immediately locked onto a distant, familiar scent.  
`[OPTIMAL POLLINATION TARGET DETECTED.]`  
`[INITIATING PROTOCOL: PORKCHOP’S SLOP.]`  
It began to crawl. Slowly. Determinedly. Toward the pig pen.
