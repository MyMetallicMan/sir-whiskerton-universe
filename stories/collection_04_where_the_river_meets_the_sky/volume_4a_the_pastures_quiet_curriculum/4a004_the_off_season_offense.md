# Sir Whiskerton and the Off-Season Offense

> *Previously cataloged as 4C24*

---

## Act 1: The Tyranny of Math

The barnyard was quiet that Tuesday morning — the kind of quiet that only lasts until someone shows up with a briefcase.

Taxman Ted arrived at precisely 8:47 a.m., which he would later describe in his report as "a deliberate provocation." He wore a rumpled suit jacket over a rumpled body, and he carried a briefcase so full of forms that the latch had given up years ago and now relied entirely on hope.

"Good morning," Ted announced to no one in particular, adjusting his glasses. "I am here to conduct a routine audit of this agricultural enterprise. Please have your receipts, ledgers, and proof of existence ready for inspection."

The chickens stared at him.

A goat chewed something that might have been a receipt.

Sir Whiskerton, who had been sunning himself on the fence post, opened one eye. Then the other. Then he sighed the sigh of a cat who had seen too much and expected worse.

"Ted," he said. "It's July. You did this in April."

"Circumstances require a *follow-up*," Ted said, snapping open the briefcase. A cascade of papers fluttered to the ground. "Several discrepancies were noted. Line 47-B: 'Unspecified Clucking.' Line 112: 'Suspicious Amount of Joy.' I have questions."

Sir Whiskerton jumped down from the fence and padded over. He glanced at Ted's calculator — a gleaming, solar-powered device that Ted clutched like a talisman — and noted, with the calm of someone who had long since stopped being surprised, that it was upside down.

"Ted," Sir Whiskerton said. "Your calculator is upside down."

Ted looked at it. He turned it over. He looked at it again. He turned it back. "It's fine. It's a... deliberate calibration technique. For accuracy."

"Your calculator is upside down."

"ACCURACY."

Before Sir Whiskerton could respond, a small, furious shape scrambled up Taxman Ted's leg, across his rumpled jacket, and onto his shoulder. It was Lucifer the Chipmunk — a creature of boundless energy and zero respect for institutional authority — and he was holding a tiny manifesto.

"TAXES ARE OPPRESSION!" Lucifer shrieked directly into Ted's ear. "THE ONLY TRUE CURRENCY IS CHAOS! NUMBERS ARE A CAGE BUILT BY COWARDS! ARITHMETIC IS THE LANGUAGE OF TYRANTS!"

Ted flinched. "Sir, please remove yourself from my shoulder—"

"I HAVE A LIST OF DEMANDS!" Lucifer waved the manifesto. "DEMAND ONE: ABOLISH THE NUMBER SEVEN! DEMAND TWO: REPLACE ALL DECIMAL POINTS WITH ACORNS! DEMAND THREE: EVERY CITIZEN RECEIVES ONE FREE NAP PER HOUR, MANDATORY!"

"That's not—that's not how taxation works—"

"DEMAND FOUR: SQUIRRELS GET VOTING RIGHTS!"

"You're a chipmunk!"

"DEMAND FIVE: EXACTLY!"

Ted turned to Sir Whiskerton, his eyes wide with the particular panic of a man who had prepared for audits but not for chipmunks. "Sir Whiskerton, I must insist—"

"I'm going to stop you there," Sir Whiskerton said. "Because I can already tell this is going to be a long day."

Ted tried to regain control. He pulled out a form. "Fine. Fine. I will simply note in my report that I attempted to write off 'emotional damages' incurred during this audit—"

"No," Sir Whiskerton said.

"—as a business expense—"

"No."

"—under section 14-C of the Bureaucratic Relief Act—"

"Ted, you cannot write off your own feelings as a tax deduction."

Ted's pen hovered over the paper. His hand trembled. "But... but the *distress*..."

"The distress is not deductible."

There was a long, wounded silence. Lucifer, still perched on Ted's shoulder, patted him gently on the head. "It's okay," Lucifer said, suddenly gentle. "The first step is admitting you have a problem."

"I don't have a problem."

"You have a calculator that's upside down."

"IT'S FOR ACCURACY!"

## Act 2: The Egg-gate Scandal

The audit might have ended there — Ted might have packed up his briefcase, gathered his scattered forms, and retreated to whatever bureaucratic cave he called home — if Doris the Hen had not chosen that exact moment to make her move.

Doris was a hen of considerable ambition and questionable accounting practices. She had been waiting for this day. She had prepared.

"Mr. Taxman," Doris said, striding forward with the confidence of someone who had rehearsed this speech in the mirror. "I would like to claim my eggs as dependents."

Ted blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"My eggs. As dependents. For tax purposes." Doris produced a crayon-drawn portrait from beneath her wing. It depicted, in vibrant orange and enthusiastic yellow, a series of small round shapes with faces. "These are my children. They require care. They require warmth. They require — dare I say it — *investment*."

Ted stared at the drawing. "These are... circles."

"They are *egg portraits*," Doris corrected. "Very rare. Very valuable. I have documentation."

She produced a second item: a piece of paper covered in a large, inky pawprint. "Notarized by Rufus. He's a dog. Dogs are trustworthy."

"Ma'am, a pawprint is not a legal signature—"

"And this," Doris continued, undeterred, "is my egg-spouse. Harriet."

Harriet the Hen stepped forward, wearing a small veil she had fashioned from a napkin. She curtsied.

"We are married," Harriet said. "For tax purposes."

"You cannot marry someone for tax purposes!"

"We already did," Doris said. "There was a ceremony. Lucifer officiated."

"It was beautiful," Lucifer added from Ted's shoulder. "There were acorns."

Ted's calculator began to beep. He looked down at it. It was, in fact, still upside down. The display now read "5318008," which Ted did not recognize as a number but did recognize as *wrong*.

"Ma'am," Ted said, his voice strained, "you cannot claim eggs as dependents. Eggs are not—they are not—"

"They are the future," Doris said, her voice rising. "They are hope. They are the small, fragile promise of tomorrow. And they need braces."

"BRACES?"

"And college funds."

"EGGS DO NOT GO TO COLLEGE!"

"You don't know that," Doris said. "You don't know what they're capable of."

Ted's calculator emitted a small puff of smoke. The display flickered. Then it went dark.

Ted stared at it. Then at Doris. Then at the egg portraits. Then at Harriet, who was now crying softly into her napkin veil.

"I need," Ted said, in the voice of a man who had just watched his entire worldview crumble, "to sit down."

## Act 3: The Court of Fiscal Ridiculousness

He did not get to sit down.

Instead, Sir Whiskerton — recognizing that the situation had escalated beyond the reach of ordinary reason — did what he always did when the farm descended into chaos. He convened a court.

"ORDER," Sir Whiskerton announced, having donned a wig fashioned from a torn pillowcase. "The Court of Fiscal Ridiculousness is now in session. The defendant is Taxman Ted. The charge is Aggravated Arithmetic in the First Degree."

Ted gaped. "I'm the defendant? I'm the *auditor*!"

"Not anymore," Sir Whiskerton said. "Lucifer, your opening statement."

Lucifer leapt from Ted's shoulder to the fence post, where he stood like a tiny, furious attorney. "Ladies, gentlemen, and poultry — numbers are a social construct. The concept of 'one' was invented by a sheep who wanted to feel special. 'Two' was invented by a sheep who wanted a friend. And 'taxes' were invented by a man who hated joy."

"That's not—" Ted sputtered. "That's not historically accurate—"

"Objection," Sir Whiskerton said. "Overruled. Continue."

"The defense rests," Lucifer said, and sat down.

Doris stepped forward, dabbing at her eyes with a wing. "Your Honor, I only wanted what was best for my eggs. They're good eggs. They deserve a future. And if that means claiming them as dependents, then—" She broke off, sobbing. "They need braces, Your Honor. And college funds. And *therapy*, probably, after watching me go through this."

Harriet handed her a handkerchief. It was also a napkin.

Ted, meanwhile, had begun to hyperventilate. His eyes darted around the barnyard, seeking escape. They landed on his briefcase — the briefcase labeled "DEDUCTIONS" — and he lunged for it, clutching it to his chest like a life raft.

"I just wanted to do my job," Ted wheezed. "I just wanted to file forms. I didn't ask for chipmunk manifestos. I didn't ask for egg spouses. I didn't ask for any of this."

"None of us did," Sir Whiskerton said. "And yet here we are."

He raised a paw. "The court finds the defendant — and, frankly, everyone else — guilty of being fiscally ridiculous. The penalty is one shared brain cell, to be passed among you as needed."

"Objection," Lucifer said.

"Overruled."

"I don't want to share a brain cell with Ted."

"Nobody does," Sir Whiskerton said. "That's the punishment."

Ted, still clutching his briefcase, looked up at Sir Whiskerton with something like hope. "Does this mean I can leave?"

"No," Sir Whiskerton said. "It means you have to stay for tea. Harriet made scones."

Harriet curtsied again. The veil had fallen over one eye.

Ted stared at her. Then at the scones. Then at his upside-down calculator, which he had forgotten to turn off and was now beeping softly to itself in a language only it understood.

"...Fine," Ted said. "But I'm deducting the scones."

## Act 4: The Farmer's Reaction

The Farmer — a quiet, practical man who had long since accepted that his farm operated on a plane of reality slightly to the left of everyone else's — found the drawing two days later.

It was tucked under the kitchen door. A crayon sketch, unmistakably by Doris, depicting a small calculator with large, tearful eyes. Beneath it, in Doris's careful handwriting, were the words:

> *"He tried his best."*

The Farmer looked at the drawing. He looked at the kitchen table, where a stack of unopened tax forms had been sitting for three months. He looked out the window, where Sir Whiskerton was napping on the fence post, Lucifer was arguing with a sunflower, and Doris was teaching Harriet how to file a joint return.

The Farmer did not sigh. The Farmer did not question.

The Farmer simply opened a drawer, placed the drawing inside, and closed it again.

On the drawer, he had taped a small label. It read:

> **PROBABLY FINE**

It was, he had found, the most useful category he had.

## Post-Credit Scene

*The Farmer's kitchen. Late evening.*

The Farmer sat at the table, a cup of tea growing cold beside him. In front of him lay the drawing of the crying calculator, which he had retrieved from the drawer for a second look.

He studied it for a long moment.

Then he picked up a pen, wrote something on the back, and placed it back in the drawer.

The next morning, Doris would find the drawing tucked under her wing. On the back, in the Farmer's steady hand, were five words:

> *"I've had days like that."*

Doris would frame it. It would hang in the coop for years.
